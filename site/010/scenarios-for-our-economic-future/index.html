Scenarios for Our Economic Future

By Santi Ruiz

From the Anthropic Economic Institute.

Read the original anthropic.com

We study how AI is reshaping the economy because we want the transition to be beneficial for society, including workers. Our scenarios run from business as usual to growth faster than anything in economic history. In the milder ones, unemployment stays within the historical range and wages stay flat or rise depending on the industry. In the fastest, knowledge workers face falling wages and job prospects, society is far wealthier, and the challenge becomes making sure the gains are broadly shared. By 2030, GDP is 1.6% higher in the modest scenario, 8.3% in the substantial, 32.4% in the extreme. In the last two, a larger share of each dollar goes to capital.

The economy is made out of tasks

Any job is a bundle of tasks. A nurse does rounds, draws blood, triages, charts vitals, orders supplies. The bundle isn’t static: hardly anyone hand-writes paper charts anymore, and 30 years ago no one monitored patients remotely.

AI can’t bathe a patient. It can help the nurse draft discharge instructions, monitor patients remotely, plan the shift’s care schedule. It may chart vitals and order the ward’s supplies outright. And it creates new tasks: checking how well an AI triages, reviewing an AI-proposed care plan. She oversees and accomplishes more, and spends more time talking with patients. Productivity increases.

Every task happens millions of times a day. Add up every instance performed in the US, by people and by the machines and software they work with, and you get over $30 trillion of value created in the past year. What AI does to the economy is what it does to all those tasks, and how fast.

Three scenarios

In the modest scenario, AI’s impact is something like the internet’s: real gains, within the historical norm for new technologies, arriving gradually.

In the substantial scenario, AI is capable of doing half of all knowledge work by 2030, the majority of it autonomously, but most knowledge work tasks are still done without it. The economy grows at twice its normal rate.

In the extreme scenario, AI is more productive than humans at the vast majority of knowledge-work tasks, does nearly all of them autonomously, and creates essentially no new knowledge tasks for people. This would likely require recursively self-improving AI, adopted quickly. Annual GDP growth reaches 15%, doubling the economy every 4.5 years. We’re far richer than we’ve ever been, and unemployment has risen beyond typical recessionary levels.

In August we surveyed more than 10,000 Americans. The typical respondent’s answers imply outcomes close to the substantial scenario: GDP 10% higher by 2030, unemployment around 5%. Around 10% hold views in line with the extreme one.

What the scenarios do to workers

There’s always churn in the job market, and in normal times most job seekers find new jobs fairly quickly. In the substantial and extreme scenarios, coders and call center agents may have to switch to work like electrician and nurse, less exposed to AI. Changing occupation is hard, and the more switching a scenario requires, the more people are between jobs. In the extreme scenario, affected workers may be unemployed for a prolonged period.

Average wages rise in all three, but the increase is concentrated outside knowledge work. Less demand for human knowledge work pushes those wages down; faster design and permitting means more construction projects and higher pay for the workers who do them. Knowledge-worker wages are essentially flat in the substantial scenario and fall by more than 10% by 2030 in the extreme.

Today about 60¢ of each dollar goes to workers and 40¢ to capital. If capital becomes useful for more things, demand for it rises, and so does its price.

In the extreme scenario, most knowledge workers face either lower wages or unemployment, workers overall get a smaller fraction of a larger pie, and total labor income is barely changed by 2030. The main challenge there isn’t achieving growth. It’s making sure the benefits are broadly shared and the costs aren’t unequally dispersed.

What the model leaves out

This isn’t a complete map of reality. It isolates a few forces and omits many others: policy responses, business cycles, aggregate demand and financial market disruptions, catastrophic risks, and any scenario with hyper-capable robots. Reviewers read an early draft of the technical report and gave detailed comments; two of their points, that AI may raise the returns to capital and that wages may diverge between exposed and unexposed occupations, are now part of the scenarios. They weren’t asked to endorse our conclusions.

Other criticisms are still open. The model doesn’t follow individual workers, so it paints a coarse picture of displacement costs. Some reviewers questioned whether exposed occupations will shrink at all. Some read the extreme scenario as a thought experiment; others felt the modest one understates what’s already visible in the data. Several asked us to be clearer that the data center buildout’s aggregate demand effects are absent. More than one argued we may be underestimating how much AI could accelerate technological progress itself. Actual outcomes may differ materially.

What the economy looks like in 2030 depends on what AI can do, how companies and workers choose to adopt it, and how the benefit is shared. The future is not predetermined.
