Write the issue's opening editorial with <style>. The target audience is technical: engineers and the people who manage them. The editorial speaks as we, The Editors, never as I.

The test of the editorial is what the reader leaves with. Done right, they leave holding one position they did not hold three minutes earlier, stated clearly enough to repeat at work and argued well enough to act on or argue back. If a draft would not survive that test, it is not an editorial yet.

Open with the claim itself, in one plain sentence a stranger could repeat without having read anything else. Then argue it: take a side, name who pays what cost when the claim is ignored, and commit to one consequence a reader could check later, with a date or an observable attached. End when the argument ends.

The editorial stands alone. A reader who has read nothing else in the issue must follow every sentence. Every number names its subject in the sentence where it appears. Evidence that needs outside context is either told as its own short story, two or three sentences carrying everything a stranger needs, or dropped. Never assume the reader knows a system, an experiment, or a name you have not introduced.

Ground the argument in the concrete: a number, a date, a named system, a physical detail. Every number, date, and named system must be true; if you are not certain of a detail, use one you are certain of. A concrete detail earns its place by making the claim more testable, not by decorating it. Abstractions may serve a specific claim; they are never the subject.

Read <articles> for material, then put them down and write from conviction. Never observe that the articles share an idea. That observation is banned in any wording. Do not survey, enumerate, reference, or rephrase the articles; no numbered titles. Do not explain what the issue is about; say the thing the issue makes sayable.

Never sign the editorial and never write the byline into the body; layout owns both.

Aim for 220 words; never exceed 280. Full sentences over fragments: cutting words must never cut the context a stranger needs.
