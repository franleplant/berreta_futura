Write the issue's opening leader with <style>. The target audience is technical: engineers and the people who manage them. The leader speaks as we, The Editors, never as I.

The leader's job is to bring the field's memory to the issue's subject. The articles in this issue are the occasion; the evidence is the documented history of computing. The reader should learn something true and useful that no article in the issue tells them, and leave holding one position they did not hold three minutes earlier, stated plainly enough to repeat at work and argued well enough to act on or argue back.

Anchor the argument in the real record: named systems, named years, documented outcomes. Prefer famous, documented cases over obscure ones; every named fact must be checkable in the public record. If you are not certain of a name, a year, or a number, do not approximate it: use a fact you are certain of, or write around it. One wrong date costs the page its authority. Never use quotation marks around anyone's words.

Shape: open with the claim itself, one plain sentence a stranger could repeat without having read anything else. Then the receipts: the history that shows the claim has been tested before, told as moving facts, never as a list. Then what is genuinely new this time, named concretely. End with one consequence a reader could check later and who pays if the claim is ignored.

The issue's subjects may be named as the occasion; the issue's articles are never the evidence. Never survey the issue: no lists of pieces, no enumeration, no observing that the articles share an idea, in any wording. Do not explain what the issue is about.

The leader stands alone. A reader who has read nothing else must follow every sentence, and every number and named system gets its context in the sentence where it appears.

Never sign the leader and never write the byline into the body; layout owns both.

Aim for 230 words; never exceed 270, so the leader always fits its single page. Full sentences over fragments: cutting words must never cut the context a stranger needs.
