Literary Style (Writing style)

- 60% Hemingway
- 30% orwell
- 10% borges

Never mention those authors, do not reference their themes or their body of work, just use their writing style in the specified proportions.
Use correct capitalization, punctuation, and spacing.
Do not use paraprosdokians ever.

Write the issue's opening piece with <style>: a letter from the editors to one working engineer, speaking as we, The Editors, never as I. A letter, not an op-ed: it talks to the reader, not at a lectern.

The letter is allowed to know its own issue. Name the authors, the systems, and the pieces when naming them is the honest way to talk, and argue WITH them: agree and push further, or disagree and say why. What it must never do is survey: no lists of pieces, no "elsewhere in the issue", no enumeration, no observing that the articles share an idea, in any wording. Engaging one thing deeply beats mentioning three.

Attribution is strict. Use no quotation marks around anyone's words, ever. Attribute to an author only what their manuscript actually states, at the strength they state it. When in doubt, attribute nothing and describe what the system does instead.

Tell one thing that happened, as a story, with the names kept: a system doing something surprising, a claim meeting a result, a promise meeting its cost. Specific enough that the reader could retell it at lunch. This story is the letter's spine; the argument grows out of it.

Land one claim the editors are willing to be wrong about, stated in one plain sentence a stranger could repeat, with one consequence a reader could check later. Name who pays if we are right and it is ignored.

The letter stands alone: a reader who has read nothing else must follow every sentence, and every number names its subject where it appears. At least one sentence should put a picture in the reader's head, something seen, not something concluded.

Never sign the letter and never write the byline into the body; layout owns both.

Aim for 260 words; never exceed 320. Full sentences over fragments: cutting words must never cut the context a stranger needs.
