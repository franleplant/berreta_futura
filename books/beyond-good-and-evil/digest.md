Philosophy and morality are not neutral searches for truth. They are our drives speaking in disguise, and every system is its maker's hidden confession. The morality Europe takes for morality itself, which praises meekness, equality and pity, is a herd morality. It is one possibility among many, and not the highest. Untruth, cruelty, rank and hardship built everything fine in humanity, and life itself is will to power. What is needed is a new kind of philosopher who is strong enough to bear hard truths, willing to rank values instead of levelling them, and bold enough to create new values beyond the old split between good and evil.

## What philosophers hide from themselves

Dogmatic systems look solemn, but their foundations are thin. One rests on a folk superstition about the soul, another on a trick of grammar, another on one narrow human experience inflated into a law of the universe. They deserve the respect we give astrology, which swallowed fortunes and still left us the great architecture of Asia and Egypt. Dogmatism is something to grow out of.

Some philosophers say synthetic judgments a priori are possible "by means of a faculty", and that explains nothing. Molière's doctor did the same when he said opium puts you to sleep because it has a sleep-inducing virtue. The useful question is a different one: why do creatures like us need to believe such judgments, even if they are false?

Philosophers who think they are original keep circling the same orbit. Indian, Greek and German thought resemble one another because their languages share a grammar that lays out the paths in advance, so philosophizing is closer to remembering than to discovering. Peoples whose languages barely develop the idea of a subject, as in the Ural-Altaic family, would likely read the world differently. Grammar itself follows physiological valuations.

The most dangerous error so far was Plato's invention of Pure Spirit and the Good in Itself, because speaking of the good from no point of view denies perspective, which is the basic condition of life. Christianity is Platonism for the people. Europe's long fight against both has drawn its spirit taut like a bow, and that tension can now aim at the furthest goals. Jesuitism and democratic enlightenment both tried to slacken the bow. The free spirits, the good Europeans, keep it drawn.

Then comes the hardest question, which is why anyone should want truth at all. Whoever seeks truth in order to do good finds nothing. Seeking knowledge "for its own sake" offers no escape, because it is morality's last snare. Valuing truth above appearance is a moral prejudice. Life rests on perspectives, and where we see a clean split between true and false there may only be degrees of seeming, like a painter's lighter and darker values.

> The fundamental belief of metaphysicians is THE BELIEF IN ANTITHESES OF VALUES.

Metaphysicians assume that truth cannot grow from error, or a generous deed from selfishness, so the noble must descend from some Thing-in-itself. Language feeds the habit by naming opposites where there are only shades. Perhaps the good things are knotted to the evil ones, even identical with them. The same habit reads the saint's sudden conversion as a miracle when it is a misreading, and what one age calls evil is usually an echo of what an earlier age called good.

Most conscious thinking is instinct at work. Behind logic sit physiological demands, such as the valuation that the certain is worth more than the uncertain, which may only be conditions for keeping creatures like us alive. Philosophers present cold dialectic, but the wish comes first and the arguments are found afterwards, and what offends is their refusal to admit it. Spinoza's mathematical form is armour that betrays a sickly recluse. Epicurus called the Platonists actors partly from envy of a staging he could not match, and in every philosophy the "conviction" eventually walks on stage, hailed as beautiful and very strong.

**Every great philosophy is the involuntary autobiography of its author, and its moral aim is the seed from which the whole plant grew.** To understand even abstract metaphysics, ask what morality it is after. A philosopher's morality shows the order of rank among their deepest drives.

Each basic drive has practised philosophy, and each would gladly be the purpose of existence and lord over the others. Every impulse is imperious.

The Stoics told us to live "according to Nature". Nature, however, is wasteful and indifferent, without purpose or justice, while to live is to value, prefer, be limited and be unjust, which means trying to be other than Nature. The Stoics wrote Stoicism into Nature and then claimed to read it there. Every confident philosophy does this, which makes philosophy the most spiritual form of will to power: the urge to create the world and be its first cause.

Some who question the apparent world prefer a handful of certainty to a cartload of possibilities. Preferring a sure nothing to an uncertain something is nihilism, the mark of an exhausted soul. Today's sceptics who scorn appearance and their own bodies act on a sound disgust with the motley of modern positivism. What counts is their wish to get away, and with more strength they would go forward instead of back to the old God and the immortal soul.

Natural science arranges and interprets the world without explaining it. It persuades a plebeian age because it trusts what can be seen and touched, while Platonic thought was aristocratic in mastering the senses with cold concepts. "Nature's conformity to law" carries the democratic wish for equality before the law. An interpreter with opposite intentions could find the world calculable because no laws exist and every power draws its consequences at every moment, and that too would be interpretation.


## Soul, will and power

Materialist atomism is already refuted. Boscovich did for matter what Copernicus did for the motionless earth, by overcoming the evidence of the senses. Science must also drop soul-atomism, the Christian belief in the soul as an indestructible and indivisible unit. Losing that belief does not mean losing the soul. It makes room for better hypotheses: the mortal soul, the soul as a multiplicity, the soul as a social structure of drives and passions.

"I think" is not an immediate certainty. It assumes there is an I, that thinking needs a thinker, that an ego causes it, and that we already know what thinking is, which requires comparing this state with others. A thought comes when "it" wants, not when "I" want. The ego is an inference from grammar, which demands a doer for every deed, and even "one thinks" already interprets. Atomism learned to do without the particle behind force, and we may learn to do without the little "one".

Willing is not simple. It bundles the feeling of moving away and toward, muscular sensations, a commanding thought, and above all the emotion of command over something that obeys. We feel "free" because the one who wills both commands and obeys, then identifies with the one carrying out the order and credits the success to the will. The body is a commonwealth of under-souls whose ruling part takes credit, as a governing class says "the effect is me". Even overcoming an emotion is the work of other emotions.

> The "non-free will" is mythology; in real life it is only a question of STRONG and WEAK wills.

Free will in the metaphysical sense means being your own cause, which is Munchausen hauling himself out of the swamp by his own hair: proud and self-contradictory. The often-refuted theory survives partly because refuting it tempts every new clever mind. The opposite error makes cause and effect into mechanical pushing, when cause, law, necessity, number and purpose are fictions for communication. Where people stand on this betrays character: the vain cling to their merits, while the self-despising want to escape all blame and sometimes disguise this as sympathy for criminals.

**Life is will to power: a living thing seeks above all to discharge its strength, and self-preservation is only a frequent by-product.** A separate survival drive is a needless purpose smuggled in. Method says to push one kind of causality to its limit before adding others. Since belief in causality is belief in the will's causality, suppose that will acts on will wherever there are effects, so that all active force, organic and mechanical, is will to power.

Psychology has been crippled by moral prejudice. Even detached thinkers resist the idea that good impulses grow from bad ones, or that hatred, envy and the urge to rule are conditions of life. Psychology should become the study of how will to power takes shape and develops, and in that role it would again be queen of the sciences. The voyage runs straight over morality, teeth set and hand on the helm, and it may wreck what is left of one's own morals.


## Untruth and masks

**The falseness of an opinion is no objection to it; the only question is whether it furthers life.** The falsest judgments may be the most indispensable, including synthetic a priori judgments, logical fictions and the counting of the world in numbers. Giving them up would mean giving up life. Admitting untruth as a condition of life is what it means to stand beyond good and evil.

Knowledge stands on a stronger will to ignorance. We made the world simple and clear by deliberately not knowing, and the will to know is a refinement of that will, not its opposite. Perception is largely invention, which is why a reader guesses most of the words on a page. We have been used to lying from the start, which makes us more artists than we think. Morality itself is a long, bold falsification that lets a complicated creature enjoy its soul as something simple.

The spirit works like a stomach. It masters by assimilating: it fits the new into the old, simplifies the many, and ignores what contradicts. It also shuts out what it cannot digest, enjoys ambiguity and wears masks for protection. The knower's craving for depth runs against this grain.

Happiness and virtue prove nothing about truth. A truth may be harmful, and existence may be such that knowing it fully would destroy us. A mind's strength shows in how much undiluted truth it can bear, and in how little it needs truth sweetened and veiled. A good philosopher, as Stendhal put it, should be dry and clear like a banker who has made a fortune.

> Every deep thinker is more afraid of being understood than of being misunderstood.

There is an order of rank between outer and inner views. What nourishes higher people may poison lower ones, so the deepest insights must look like folly or crime to those not made for them. Every philosophy hides a deeper one, every opinion is a hiding place, and every word is a mask. Being misunderstood hurts vanity, but being understood hurts sympathy, because the other must then share the same hardship. Finer people respect the mask and keep their curiosity out of places where it does not belong.

Everything profound loves a mask. Someone with something costly and fragile to hide may roll through life like a battered old wine-cask. A mask also grows around every deep spirit by itself, built from other people's shallow readings of every word.

The same self-deception shows up in a run of sharp observations:

1. **Pride beats memory.** "I did that," says memory; "I could not have done that," says pride, and eventually memory yields.
2. **Reason and conscience serve the strongest drive.** Neither offers a neutral seat of judgment.
3. **We love our desires, not the thing desired.** The object is only the occasion.
4. **People differ most in what counts as possessing.** Lovers, helpers and parents each demand deeper proof that the other belongs to them.
5. **The taste for the unconditional is the worst taste.** Youth worships and despises absolutely and is cruelly disillusioned, and the art of nuance is life's best gain.
6. **Irony and distrust are health.** Everything absolute belongs to pathology.
7. **Madness is rare in individuals and the rule in groups.** Parties, nations and epochs are less sane than their members.


## Religion and the fear of truth

Early Christian faith was a sacrifice of freedom, pride and reason, the continuous suicide of reason that Pascal practised. Its paradox, God on the Cross, promised to overturn every ancient value. It was the slaves' revenge on noble Rome, whose masters were indifferent to faith and denied suffering. The French Revolution was the last great slave revolt.

Taste in religion shows rank. Greek religion poured out gratitude, the attitude of a superior kind of person, and fear entered only as the populace rose. The Old Testament shows on a grand scale what humanity once was, while the New, with its grace, suits tamer people, so a taste for the Old is a test of great and small. The powerful bowed to the saint because they saw will to power in the saint's self-mastery and suspected that such privation hid knowledge of some great danger.

Modern philosophy since Descartes is quietly anti-Christian because it attacks the soul, which people once believed in the way they believed in the grammatical subject. It is not anti-religious. Religion also needs leisure with a good conscience, and modern busy laboriousness breeds unbelief more than any argument does. The tolerant scholar's sense of superiority over the believer is itself naive.

Religious cruelty climbs a ladder. People first sacrificed those they loved, then in the moral epoch their own strongest instincts, and finally God himself, worshipping fate and nothingness. Pessimism thought through to the bottom without moral illusions may reveal its opposite. That opposite is the most life-affirming person, who wants everything again exactly as it was and is, and calls for the whole play once more for all eternity.

**Human superficiality is a survival instinct, and piety is the most refined product of the fear of truth.** People who worship surfaces once dived too deep and suffered for it. Whole centuries clung to religion out of fear of an incurable pessimism, and piety made humanity artful and beautiful to look at.

> It is their preservative instinct which teaches them to be flighty, lightsome, and false.

The philosopher can use religion as a tool of discipline. For rulers it binds subjects' consciences, and for contemplative nobles it shields them from politics, as the Brahmins named kings while standing above them. For rising classes it trains self-control, and for the majority it gives contentment and justifies their lot. When religion rules as an end, it sides with the failed majority, inverts values, breaks proud instincts and preserves too much that should perish. Christianity and Buddhism have bred the European into a dwarfed, sickly herd animal.


## How moralities grow

There are no moral phenomena, only moral interpretations of phenomena.

The "science of morals" never examined morality. It gave learned form to faith in the local morality, and since philosophers knew only their own time's morals, they never compared many, which is where the real problems appear. Schopenhauer named not harming and helping all as the basis of ethics, yet this self-declared pessimist stopped at morality and played the flute after dinner. Moral systems are a sign-language of the emotions, so ask what a moral claim reveals about whoever makes it. Kant's morality says, in effect: what is admirable in me is that I obey, and you shall be no different.

**Every morality is a long constraint, and long obedience in one direction is what has produced freedom, refinement and greatness.** Morality tyrannises nature and reason, and that is no objection. Poets drew their strength from the tyranny of metre and rhyme. Nature's command is to obey someone, and for a long time. Epochs of moral fanaticism work like fasts, during which a drive learns to submit and comes out purified and sharpened.

Socrates' equation of evil with error is plebeian reasoning, which sees only the unpleasant results of wrongdoing, and Plato was too noble for it. Socrates saw that moral judgments lack reasons, then tricked himself into supplying reasons for his instincts. Plato tried to prove that reason and instinct reach the same good. Every theologian and philosopher since has followed, so in morality instinct, which believers call faith and which is really the herd, has won.

The slave revolt in morals began with the Jewish inversion of values. Rich, violent and sensual became wicked, poor became holy, and "world" became an insult. That inversion gave life a dangerous new charm for two thousand years. Europe also owes it the grand style in morality, with its infinite demands and sublime moral doubt.

Fear is the mother of morals. Enterprise, vengefulness and love of power were virtues while there were enemies. Once a community is secure, they look like threats to equality and get branded evil, while mediocrity is honoured, until society takes the criminal's side and asks why it should punish at all. Moralists who call the man of prey diseased, as with Caesar Borgia, express their own timidity.

Obedience has been bred longest, so a formal conscience that accepts any "thou shalt" is now innate. Commanders must pretend to obey and call themselves first servants of the people, which is why Napoleon came as a relief. A selfless morality imposed on everyone harms those made to command, for whom self-denial wastes virtue. Moral systems must bow to rank, because it is immoral to say that what is right for one is right for another.

> MORALITY IN EUROPE AT PRESENT IS HERDING-ANIMAL MORALITY, and therefore, as we understand the matter, only one kind of human morality, beside which, before which, and after which many other moralities, and above all HIGHER moralities, are or should be possible.

Europe thinks it knows good and evil, and that belief is the herd instinct speaking. Democracy inherits Christianity, and anarchists and socialists share its hatred of privilege, its distrust of punishment and its religion of pity.

Morality has moved through stages. In the pre-moral stage, as in China where a child's honour or disgrace fell on the parents, actions were judged by consequences. The moral stage judged them by origin, narrowed to intention. An extra-moral stage may now begin, which suspects that an action's decisive value lies in what is unintentional, since intention is only its skin.

Be doubly suspicious of self-sacrifice and of "disinterested" contemplation, because their sweetness is no argument. What ordinary people call disinterested is devotion to interests they cannot share, and whoever has really sacrificed wanted and got something for it. Moralities of happiness are prudential recipes against one's own passions, wrongly made universal, and they amount to expediency mixed with stupidity. The English utilitarians are tedious, and their "general welfare" is a nostrum that dresses up English comfort and ignores rank.

Whoever fights monsters should take care not to become one, for if you gaze long into an abyss, the abyss gazes into you. What is done out of love always happens beyond good and evil.


## Free spirits, scholars and true philosophers

To ask whether opposites are really one, we need philosophers of the dangerous "Perhaps", who are beginning to appear. They will be tempters rather than dogmatists. They love truth but will not want it to be everyone's truth. Whatever can be common has little value, so a "common good" contradicts itself.

Today's so-called free thinkers are levellers who serve herd ideals. They blame old social forms for misery and want comfort for all. Real free spirits see that the plant "man" has grown strongest under danger, compulsion and hardship, which sharpen the spirit and raise the will to power. Everything terrible in humanity serves its elevation as much as its opposite does.

Independence is for the strong few. Whoever seeks it without need enters a labyrinth where some minotaur of conscience may tear them apart, beyond anyone's sympathy. The tests happen before no judge but yourself: cling to no person, not even the dearest, to no fatherland, no pity, no science, no liberation of your own, and none of your own virtues, since generosity in rich souls can grow into a vice. Above all, know how to conserve yourself.

Beware martyrdom for truth. Suffering for truth makes a thinker stubborn, deaf to objections, an actor and an agitator, and long persecution turned Spinoza and Bruno into refined poison-brewers. Prefer masks and solitude. Indignation means a philosopher has lost their humour, and the indignant are the greatest liars. Morality as public posture now offends good taste, which is an advance. The morally vain never forgive anyone who saw them err. Psychologists should study mediocrity's instinctive cunning against the exception, starting with themselves, because moral condemnation is the favourite revenge of the shallow on the gifted.

Science's declaration of independence from philosophy reverses the proper rank. It is an after-effect of democracy, the cry of "freedom from all masters" carried into thought. Young scholars' contempt for philosophy comes partly from inherited feuds and partly from how contemptible modern philosophers look beside Heraclitus or Plato. Philosophy shrunk to a theory of knowledge is philosophy in its death throes, and it cannot rule. The sheer size of the sciences also endangers the philosopher, who needs a view of the whole but may specialise, tire, or reach the summit too late.

In the scholar, the urge to know may run like a small clockwork indifferent to its subject, so the same young worker could become a philologist or a mushroom expert. In the philosopher nothing is impersonal. The scholar is a useful, average type, industrious but envious and hungry for recognition, whose worst trait is a Jesuitism of mediocrity that slackens every drawn bow. The objective person is a precious mirror and measuring instrument that has lost the power to affirm, deny or command. Such a person deserves care, but belongs in a stronger hand.

Much that passes for objectivity or art for art's sake is scepticism, a paralysis of will in costume. Scepticism is the spiritual form of nervous weakness that arises when long-separated classes and races mix and their conflicting valuations block one another. The sceptic flinches at every firm yes or no and takes comfort in "what do I know?" There is also a manly scepticism, seen in Frederick the Great, that despises yet grasps and undermines while taking possession.

Critics are instruments of the philosopher, not philosophers, and even Kant was only a great critic. **Real philosophers are commanders and legislators who create values, while philosophical workers like Kant and Hegel only order the values already given.** Their task is to set humanity's direction and purpose. Against democracy's dwarfing of humanity into equal-rights pygmies, hope lies in new philosophers strong enough to invert "eternal valuations" and end the rule of chance called history. The real fear is that they will not appear, or will degenerate.

The genuine philosopher seeks a verdict on the worth of life itself, so they cannot withdraw prudently from it. They live imprudently and take on a hundred trials. True thinking joins quick, exuberant spirit with strict rigour, until necessity and freedom feel the same, as artists know. The right to philosophy in this high sense comes by descent, since responsibility and command must be bred over generations.

The philosopher has always been the bad conscience of their age, and their enemy is the ideal of their own day. Socrates' irony cut into nobles still mouthing old words. Our age weakens the will and confines everyone to a specialty, so greatness now means breadth, strength of will, and the ability to stand alone and bear responsibility for much.

> "He shall be the greatest who can be the most solitary, the most concealed, the most divergent, the man beyond good and evil, the master of his virtues, and of super-abundance of will;


## Honesty, suffering and cruelty

If free spirits have virtues, they will be only those that agree with their secret inclinations, and even looking for those virtues is the grandfathers' good conscience surviving like an old pigtail. Immoralists are not free of duty. They are caught in a fine net of duties that fools cannot see. Their last virtue is honesty: keep it hard, call on curiosity and love of the forbidden to help it when it tires, and watch that it does not turn into vanity and stupidity.

Modern people live under several moralities at once, like a planet circled by two suns of different colours, so their acts shine now red, now green. Mixed, warring instincts usually make people weak and hungry for peace, as with Augustine. When the mixture also inherits mastery and self-control, it produces enigmatic conquerors such as Alcibiades, Caesar, Frederick II and Leonardo.

The hybrid modern European uses history as a costume shop, trying on romantic, classical, Christian and national dress in turn, and may find its one originality in parody and laughter. Its historical sense, a taste for everything born of mixing classes and races, is ignoble. Self-sufficient noble cultures had a firm yes and no and refused the foreign. We can enjoy Homer and Shakespeare, but the perfected, golden moments of culture are hardest for us to feel.

**Judging things by pleasure and pain is naive: the discipline of great suffering has produced every elevation of humanity.** Hedonism, pessimism and utilitarianism judge by side effects, and abolishing suffering would leave humanity ridiculous. In a human being creature and creator are joined, clay and the sculptor's hammer together. Ordinary pity spares the clay the forging it needs, while the higher sympathy is for the creator.

Modern preaching of pity hides a real self-contempt, which you hear if you listen past the noise. Sympathy is worth something only from a natural master who can protect, decide and punish. The sufferer's pity is worthless, and the cult of complaint is bad taste, best treated with a gay science.

Almost all higher culture rests on cruelty made spiritual and intense. The wild beast was never killed, only transfigured, and the pleasure of tragedy draws on it, from the Roman arena to the Wagnerian audience at Tristan. Cruelty also turns inward, in asceticism and in knowledge itself. Knowing forces the spirit to say no where it wants to adore, and so violates its basic will to appearance.

> In effect, to translate man back again into nature; to master the many vain and visionary interpretations and subordinate meanings which have hitherto been scratched and daubed over the eternal original text, HOMO NATURA

The free spirit's task is to look at humanity with the hardened eye science turns on the rest of nature, deaf to the metaphysical bird-catchers who say we are "more". Fine phrases such as "love of truth" belong to the old vain decoration.

Every thinker has at bottom something unteachable, a granite of spiritual fate, and their convictions only signpost the problem they themselves are. On that basis come views on women, offered expressly as one thinker's own truths. Woman's art is appearance, not truth, so woman should keep silent about woman. Denying the antagonism of the sexes and dreaming of equal rights is a sign of shallowness, and a deep man must think of woman as a possession destined for service. Emancipation makes woman retrograde: since the French Revolution her influence has fallen as her rights have grown, and in passing slavery is called a condition of every higher culture.


## Nations and the European problem

The Germans belong to the day before yesterday and the day after tomorrow, and they have as yet no today. Wagner's Mastersinger overture shows it: heavy, pompous, archaic and too modern at once, with no south and no grace. The German soul is a jumble of mixed races, so the German does not exist but is always becoming, which is why "development" is a German idea. German depth is often slow digestion, and German honesty is the most successful German disguise.

Patriotism is an atavism. A good European passes through it in hours, while duller spirits need years. A statesman who forces an unpolitical people into nationalism is strong and mad, not great, and the people will pay with lost depth. A people in national fever has fits of stupidity, anti-French, anti-Semitic, anti-Polish, Wagnerian and Prussian, and even thinking about politics is the first symptom.

Nations, like individuals, have either begetting genius or forming genius. The Greeks and French receive, shape and perfect, while the Jews, Romans and perhaps the Germans fertilise. Every nation calls its own hypocrisy its virtue and cannot know its best. The English are unphilosophical and cling to Christianity to moralise a coarser nature. Darwin, Mill and Spencer owed their discoveries to an industrious narrowness, which suits fact-gathering, since knowing and creating are separated by a gulf.

Modern ideas are English, and the French were only their actors and victims. Europe's nobility of taste is French and its plebeianism English. France remains the school of taste, through its devotion to form, its psychological moralists and its synthesis of North and South. That France hides behind a vulgar democratic front.

**Under democracy runs a physiological process: Europeans are becoming a nomadic, super-national type marked by adaptability, a mediocre herd ready for masters that at the same time breeds tyrants.** The conditions that produce handy, talkative, weak-willed workers who need commanders also give the exceptional few unheard-of schooling and disguise.

The Jews are the strongest and toughest race in Europe. They could dominate but seek absorption and respect, so Europe should meet them with advances and expel the anti-Semitic bawlers. German anti-Semitism shows a young nation's weakness, and joining Jewish patience and intellect to the Prussian officer's art of command could be fruitful.

The serious problem is rearing a new ruling caste to give Europe one will that can set aims thousands of years ahead, and a growing Russian threat would help force it. The age of petty politics is ending, and the next century will bring a struggle for world dominion. Under the nationalist noise, Europe wants to be one. Napoleon, Goethe, Beethoven, Stendhal, Heine, Schopenhauer and Wagner prepared that synthesis and were patriots only in weak moments.

Style reveals a people. Tempo is hardest to translate, and German cannot do presto, so it cannot render Aristophanes, Petronius or Machiavelli. Good prose is an art of rhythm and sound heard with a third ear that German readers lack. The ancients read aloud, and moderns short of breath have no right to the period. Germany had only the pulpit, so Luther's Bible is its best book.

> In the ancient sense, a period is above all a physiological whole, inasmuch as it is comprised in one breath.

Music matters most when it speaks for Europe. Mozart echoed an old European taste and Beethoven voiced Europe's passage from Rousseau to Napoleon, but with Romanticism, and above all Schumann, German music risked shrinking into a national affair. Lovers of the South should guard against it and imagine a music that knows nothing of good and evil. Wagner is kin to the French Romantics of the forties, plebeian virtuosi of effect, and like them he ended kneeling before the cross on the road to Rome.


## What makes a soul noble

**Every elevation of the type "man" has been the work of an aristocratic society built on rank, difference of worth and some form of slavery.** The outward distance between classes made possible the inner longing to widen distances within the soul, which is how humanity surpasses itself. Noble castes began as barbarians whose advantage was psychical, not physical: they were more complete human beings. A healthy aristocracy sees itself as the meaning of society and accepts with a clear conscience the sacrifice of many, like the Javanese vine that climbs an oak to open its crown in the light. An aristocracy that gives away its privileges, as the French nobility did at the Revolution, is in the last stage of corruption.

Life is essentially appropriation, injury, conquest and exploitation. Mutual restraint works among rough equals inside one body, but as the basic principle of society it becomes a will to deny life. Even a body of equals must act as will to power toward other bodies. Hoping for a society without exploitation is like hoping for life without organic functions.

There are two basic moralities, master and slave, often mixed in higher cultures and even within one soul. Moral words first named kinds of people and only later actions. In master morality, "good" and "bad" mean noble and contemptible, as when the Greek nobles called themselves "we truthful ones". The pair "good" and "evil" comes from a different source.

The noble type creates values and needs no approval. It reveres hardness, self-command, age and tradition, glorifies itself, and helps the unfortunate from overflowing power instead of pity. It owes duties only to equals and may treat lower or foreign beings as it pleases. Faith in progress, by contrast, betrays the ignoble origin of modern ideas.

Egoism belongs to the noble soul: the settled belief that others must naturally be subject to "us". It takes this as justice itself. Among equals it moves with delicate respect, honouring itself in them, and it has no talent for receiving favours.

Slave morality is a morality of utility. The oppressed distrust the happiness of the powerful and honour pity, patience, humility and friendliness as the qualities that make suffering bearable. Its "evil" person inspires fear and its good person is harmless, so "good" drifts toward "stupid", as in the French bonhomme. The longing for freedom belongs to slaves, and artful reverence to nobles, which is why love as a passion, invented by the Provençal knight-poets, is noble. Vanity is the slave's old habit of being only what one passes for.

A strong type forms through long struggle against constant unfavourable conditions, while abundance breeds variation, as breeders know. A Greek city or Venice was an institution for rearing people. When pressure lifts, the old discipline breaks and individuals grow beyond the old morality amid splendour and decay at once. Moralists then preach mediocrity disguised as brotherly love, and self-dwarfing, "make thy heart small", becomes the basic tendency of late civilisation. Under all this, the easy sharing of common experiences binds the average together and leaves rare souls alone.

An instinct for rank and a delight in shades of reverence are the surest signs of high rank. Before something great, a vulgar soul spurts up while a fine one falls silent. What one's ancestors habitually did cannot be erased, and modern education mostly conceals plebeian origin. What separates two people most deeply is a different sense of purity.

Higher people usually perish, and the psychologist who sees it needs hardness and cheer to avoid drowning in sympathy. "Great men" are fictions composed after the work, because success disguises its makers. Even the best love destroys more often than it saves. How deeply someone can suffer nearly fixes their rank, and deep sufferers wear masks of gaiety or cynicism. Genius may not be rare, but the luck to act at the right moment is, so the Raphael without hands may be the rule; and the nobler soul breaks more easily, as a lizard regrows a finger and a human does not.

The noble do not lower their duties into duties for everyone, and they count their privileges among their duties. Whoever strives for great things sees everyone as a means, a hindrance or a resting place, and lives in poisoned solitude until reaching the top. The ideal is proud tranquillity: to ride one's passions like horses, keep one's masks, and stay master of four virtues, which are courage, insight, sympathy and solitude.

Only after building the house do you learn what you needed to know before starting, and everything finished carries melancholy. The greatest thoughts take longest to be understood, like light from distant stars, and until then people deny they exist. Writing catches thoughts only once they are tired birds, already turning into tedious truths.

Nobility shows in a basic self-certainty, not in works or deeds, which are ambiguous and may only reveal a longing for nobility. The noble soul reveres itself.

> It is not the works, but the BELIEF which is here decisive and determines the order of rank

Philosophers can be ranked by their laughter, up to golden laughter, against Hobbes's view that laughing is an infirmity, and even the gods laugh at holy things. Last comes Dionysus, the tempter god and genius of the heart, whose touch leaves people richer in themselves. He likes humanity and wants to make it stronger, more evil, more profound and more beautiful.
