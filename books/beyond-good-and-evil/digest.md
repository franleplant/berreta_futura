Nietzsche's book is an attack on the philosophers' faith in truth and in the moral opposites they built on it. He argues that every great system is a disguised confession, that logic and morality both rest on instinct, and that the instinct under everything is a will to power. The morality of modern Europe, with its pity, equality and love of safety, is in his eyes a herd morality. It is one morality among several possible ones, though it claims to be the only one. Against it he calls for a new kind of philosopher who creates values instead of tidying old ones, and for a higher type of human being to receive them. Going "beyond good and evil" is his name for that task. Read the first chapter and the last in full. The middle is uneven, but nearly every page has something sharp in it.

## Why the philosophers are suspect

Nietzsche opens with a question he thinks no philosopher has seriously asked: why do we want truth at all? "Granted that we want the truth: WHY NOT RATHER untruth? And uncertainty? Even ignorance?" He compares the moment to Oedipus before the Sphinx, where it is no longer clear which party is asking. He calls raising the question possibly the greatest risk a thinker can take. Even the ideal of disinterested knowledge does not escape him. "Knowledge for its own sake," he says, is morality's last snare, because it is itself a moral valuation.

The basic prejudice of metaphysicians, he says, is their faith in opposite values. They reasoned that truth cannot come from error, or the generous deed from selfishness, so these things must have a separate source in the Thing-in-itself or a hidden God. They claimed to doubt everything and never doubted this. Nietzsche suggests the popular oppositions may be "frog perspectives," views taken from below. Perhaps what makes the good things valuable is their being knotted to the bad things they seem to oppose. Pretence, delusion and selfishness may be worth more to life than their honoured opposites. Pursuing this needs a new philosopher, a philosopher of the dangerous "Perhaps."

Most conscious thought, philosophy included, is steered by instinct. Being conscious plays as small a part in thinking as the act of birth plays in heredity. Behind logic stand physiological demands for keeping a certain kind of life going. A valuation like "the certain is worth more than the uncertain" may be necessary for creatures like us and still be superficial. Man may not be the measure of things.

From here Nietzsche reaches his most startling claim. A false opinion is not refuted by being false. The question is whether it furthers life. Synthetic judgments a priori, logical fictions and the counterfeiting of the world by numbers may all be false and indispensable, and to give them up would be to reject life. A philosophy that admits untruth as a condition of life has already placed itself beyond good and evil.

The preference for truth over appearance is, he says, only a moral prejudice. There are degrees of semblance, like the lighter and darker tones in a painting, and if you abolished the "seeming world" you would have no truth left. The philosopher has a duty of distrust, including distrust of grammar. Perception is mostly invention. The senses resist the new. Germans heard ARCUBALISTA and made it ARMBRUST. A reader takes in about five words in twenty and guesses the rest. Nietzsche notices that he sees a speaker's face more sharply than his eyesight allows, so he must be making it up. We are more artists than we know. Even dreams count: a man who often flies in his sleep will long for happiness differently, and will find the poets' "flight" too earthly.

Philosophers pretend to follow pure dialectic. In fact they defend a prior wish with reasons found afterward. Kant's stiff Tartuffery leads him to the categorical imperative. Spinoza's mathematical form is armour that betrays a sickly recluse's timidity. Dogmatic systems rested on things like the soul-superstition or a trick of grammar. Dogmatism was a youthful stage, like astrology, which consumed more labour than any real science and still gave us the architecture of Asia and Egypt.

This leads to one of the book's best insights. Every great philosophy is "a species of involuntary and unconscious auto-biography." To understand a metaphysics, ask what morality it is aiming at, because each drive wants to rule the others and a system shows their order of rank. The scholar is different. In him the drive to know may be a small clockwork running apart from his real interests, and whether he studies philology, mushrooms or chemistry hardly matters. Under all learning lies "a granite of spiritual fate." That is why Nietzsche later calls his views on woman only "my truths."

Philosophy always creates the world in its own image. It is the most spiritual form of will to power. The Stoics claimed to live according to Nature, but they wanted Nature according to the Stoa. In any case, living means valuing, preferring and being unjust and limited, which is what Nature, in its indifference, is not. Plato's Pure Spirit and Good in Itself were the most dangerous error, because they denied perspective, the basic condition of life. Speaking as a physician, Nietzsche wonders whether Socrates corrupted Plato and earned his hemlock. Christianity is Platonism for the people. The long fight against it has strung Europe's spirit like a bow. Jesuitism and democratic enlightenment have tried to unbend it. The free spirits keep it taut, and with such a bow one can aim at the furthest goals.


## Grammar and the self

Kant asked how synthetic judgments a priori are possible and answered "by means of a faculty." Nietzsche compares this to Molière's doctor, who explains that opium makes us sleep because of its virtus dormitiva, and pictures young Tübingen theologians running off to find new faculties. The better question is why belief in such judgments is necessary. They may be false, yet creatures like us may need them.

Philosophies grow along a scheme laid down by grammar. That is why Indian, Greek and German thought resemble each other, and why speakers of Ural-Altaic languages, with a weak sense of the subject, might see the world otherwise. To philosophize is in part to remember.

"I think" is no immediate certainty. It assumes there is an ego, that the ego causes thinking, and that we already know what thinking is. The same applies to Schopenhauer's "I will." A thought comes when "it" wishes, not when "I" wish. Even "one thinks" adds an interpretation, and we may learn to do without the little "one" as physics did without the material atom. Since Descartes, modern philosophy has been secretly anti-Christian, because it attacks the soul by attacking the grammatical subject. It treats the "I" as a synthesis made by thinking. This is compatible with religion, as the Vedanta shows, but not with Christianity's basic premise.

Soul-atomism, the belief in an indivisible soul, should be expelled from science, as Boscovich expelled the material atom and Copernicus the stationary earth. The soul hypothesis can still be refined into a "mortal soul," a "soul of subjective multiplicity," or a "soul as social structure of the instincts and passions."

Willing is a bundle of sensations, a commanding thought, and above all the emotion of command. In willing we both command and obey, but the word "I" hides this, so we think willing alone produces action. The willer takes credit for the work of the "under-souls," like a governing class claiming the commonwealth's successes: L'EFFET C'EST MOI. Free will is the feeling of supremacy over what obeys. The will to overcome an emotion is only the will of other emotions.

Metaphysical free will would require being causa sui, which Nietzsche likens to Münchhausen pulling himself from the swamp by his own hair. Determinism errs the other way, treating cause and effect as real forces instead of useful fictions. "In real life it is only a question of STRONG and WEAK wills." The free-will doctrine survives partly because it is refutable, and someone always feels strong enough to refute it. People take sides from personal motives. The vain cling to responsibility. The self-contemptuous want to escape it, and dress their fatalism up as sympathy for criminals.

"Nature's conformity to law" is an interpretation coloured by democratic instinct, the hidden motto being "Ni dieu, ni maître." An opposite reader could see the same regularity as every power drawing its consequences at every moment. Nietzsche admits this is also interpretation and welcomes the objection. Physics arranges the world and does not explain it. It convinces by a plebeian trust in what can be seen and touched, where Plato's grey conceptual nets were aristocratic, though the modern way may suit a future race of builders. The idealist who says the world is the work of our organs refutes himself, since our organs would then be the work of our organs.


## The will to power

A living thing seeks above all to discharge its strength. Life itself is will to power, and self-preservation is only one frequent result of it. Positing self-preservation first is a superfluous teleology, which Nietzsche blames on Spinoza. He states a "morality of method": do not assume several kinds of causality until one has been pushed to its limit. If we believe in the causality of the will at all, we must test whether will is the only cause, and read mechanical action as will acting on will.

Our desires and passions are the only reality given to us, and thinking is a relation among drives. So Nietzsche proposes that the material world is a more primitive form of the same world of drives. If instinct, nutrition and generation all trace back to one basic will, we may call all force will to power. "The world seen from within" would be that and nothing else.

Psychology, so understood, becomes the queen of the sciences. Moral prejudice has kept it from studying how good impulses come from bad ones, and how hatred, envy and imperiousness are conditions of life. Nietzsche pictures a ship sailing straight over morality and tells the reader to set their teeth and hold the helm. The psychologist must infer hidden forces as astronomers infer dark bodies near the sun. Scholars lose their nose when the "big hunt" begins. To understand religious experience you would need Pascal's bruised depth and a clear, wicked spirituality, and so you must do everything yourself.

The will to knowledge rests on a stronger will to ignorance and untruth, of which it is a refinement. The spirit works like a stomach. It assimilates the new to the old, simplifies, and seeks the feeling of growing power. It also likes to shut windows, enjoy ambiguity and wear masks. The knower who insists on depth works against this and practises a kind of intellectual cruelty. The task, then, is to translate man back into nature, scraping the flattering paint off the text "homo natura" and facing man "with fearless Oedipus-eyes, and stopped Ulysses-ears." Nietzsche admits the task is strange and that he has no better answer to "why knowledge at all?"


## Masks, hardness and the free spirit

What nourishes higher people may poison lower ones. The true line between exoteric and esoteric is between looking up and looking down. Some books unsettle weak souls and summon the brave, and books for the general reader smell of paltry people. A deep thinker fears being understood more than being misunderstood, because to be understood means another must suffer what he suffered.

"Every profound spirit needs a mask." Someone with something fragile to hide may roll through life like an old, hooped wine-cask. Another, after a generous act, thrashes the witness to blur the memory. Human superficiality is a preservative instinct, and an excessive love of surfaces shows someone who has been below them, like the "burnt children" among artists. How deeply a person can suffer nearly fixes their rank, and deep sufferers hide behind gaiety, science, or the cynicism of Hamlet and Galiani. Refined humanity respects the mask. Every philosophy hides another one, and behind every cave lies a deeper cave.

"Happiness and virtue are no arguments." Neither are unhappiness and harm. Something could be true and highly dangerous, and existence might be such that full knowledge of it destroys a person. The amiable idealists let the good, true and beautiful swim together in one pond. Whoever seeks truth only to do good, as in Voltaire's maxim, will find nothing. A mind's strength might then be measured by how much truth it can bear without softening. Severity and craft may serve truth better than good nature. Stendhal said a philosopher should be dry, clear and without illusion, like a banker who has made a fortune. Philosophers should avoid martyrdom. Long persecution turned Spinoza and Bruno into poison-brewers and turns the martyr-philosopher into a stage-bawler. Better a good solitude and a mask.

The coming philosophers will be tempters and experimenters. They will not want their truth to be everyone's. "My opinion is MY opinion: another person has not easily a right to it." Independence is dangerous. "It is the business of the very few to be independent; it is a privilege of the strong." Whoever chooses it without need enters a labyrinth where a minotaur of conscience may destroy them unseen. Such a person must test themselves by cleaving to nothing: not a person, a country, a science, their own liberation, or their own virtues, such as hospitality, the danger of rich souls. The taste for the unconditional is the worst taste, and belongs to youth. Nuance is life's best gain.

The so-called free spirits of the day are levellers seeking the "green-meadow happiness of the herd." Their doctrines are equal rights and sympathy with all sufferers. A "common good" is a contradiction, since what can be common is of small value. Against them Nietzsche holds that the plant "man" has grown best under danger and hardship, and that everything terrible in man serves the species as much as its opposite.

Honesty is the free spirits' last virtue, and they must call up all their devils to serve it, while guarding against its becoming vanity or stupidity, "stupid to the point of sanctity," as the Russians say. A philosopher must leave his citadel to study the average man. Cynics like Galiani, who talk of man as a belly with two needs, are instructive. The indignant man is the greatest liar.

Then come aphorisms, the easiest part of the book to sample. "There is no such thing as moral phenomena, but only a moral interpretation of phenomena." What an age calls evil is an echo of an older good. What is done out of love lies beyond good and evil. "He who fights with monsters should be careful lest he thereby become a monster. And if thou gaze long into an abyss, the abyss will also gaze into thee." Irony and distrust are healthy, and the absolute is pathological. Madness is rare in individuals and the rule in parties, nations and epochs. Memory says "I did that," pride says "I could not have," and memory yields. Great men come from the duration of great feelings, not their strength. Maturity is regaining a child's seriousness at play. What is explained stops concerning us, which casts a strange light on "Know thyself." Life's great turns come when we rebaptize our badness as our best. Reason and conscience both serve the strongest drive. We love our knowledge less once we share it. "One loves ultimately one's desires, not the thing desired." And the French Revolution, a farce close up, was read from afar by all Europe until the text disappeared under the interpretation.


## The religious mood

Early Christian faith was no simple slave-faith. It belonged to refined, sceptical minds who had to sacrifice freedom, pride and reason to the absurd, a continuous suicide of reason. Pascal's faith resembles a worm-like reason that cannot be killed at one blow. "God on the Cross" promised a transvaluation of ancient values. The miracle of the saint, a bad man suddenly made good, may be a misreading. Solitude, fasting and swings between sensuality and penance looked like moral opposites because psychology believed in moral opposites. Schopenhauer's question "how is the saint possible?" and the Salvation Army belong here.

The slaves revolted against their masters' smiling indifference to faith and suffering. Roman toleration seemed to deny the slave's hidden pain, and the slave craves the unconditioned. This runs from Christianity's victory over Rome to the French Revolution.

Religious cruelty climbs a ladder. First came sacrifices of the firstborn, then the ascetic's sacrifice of his own nature, and last the sacrifice of God for nothingness, which Nietzsche reserves for his own generation. The mighty bowed to the saint because they sensed in his self-conquest a will to power stronger than theirs.

Pessimism thought through beyond morality turns into its opposite: the world-affirming man who wants everything again as it was and is, for all eternity, and cries "da capo." This is an early form of eternal recurrence. "God" and "sin" may one day look like a child's toys and pains, and man will need new ones.

Piety is the most refined product of the fear of truth, an artistic falsification of existence before man can bear it. The Greeks' religion poured out gratitude toward life, the mark of a high type. Fear entered when the populace prevailed. Loving mankind for God's sake is the noblest feeling yet reached, since love of man without a higher aim is folly.

Theism is declining in Europe while the religious instinct grows. God's worst flaw is that he cannot make himself clear. Busy modern work breeds unbelief, as among German Protestants in trading towns who attend rites without feeling. The scholar's tolerant superiority to the religious is naive. Unbelief means revolt against race among Catholic Latins and a return to race among Northerners. Nietzsche first marked Renan's piety "la niaiserie religieuse par excellence" and came to enjoy it.

The philosopher of the future will use religion as a tool of selection. For rulers, as with the Brahmins, it binds consciences. For rising classes it trains self-control. For the many it makes their lot endurable. But religions that rule as ends in themselves "have preserved too much THAT WHICH SHOULD HAVE PERISHED." Siding with sufferers, they broke the strong instincts and bred a stunted European, Pascal being the case. Nietzsche imagines an artist with a divine hammer cursing the bunglers who spoiled the finest stone. The higher a type, the less likely it is to succeed. A taste for the Old Testament's grandeur is a touchstone, and binding it to the rococo New Testament was a sin against the spirit. The English, an unphilosophical people, need Christianity's discipline, and Methodism's "moral grunting" may be the most humanity drunkards can reach.


## The herd and its morality

A science of morals should for now be modest: collect and compare many moralities toward a typology. Philosophers knew only their own and never saw morality as a problem. Their "grounding" of morality was only learned faith in it. Schopenhauer had his principle "neminem laede" and played the flute every day after dinner. The English utilitarians, walking in Bentham's footsteps as he walked in Helvétius's, dressed up English comfort as universal virtue. "General welfare" is a nostrum, and one morality for all harms higher men.

"In short, systems of morals are only a SIGN-LANGUAGE OF THE EMOTIONS." Kant's means: what is admirable in me is that I obey, and you shall too. Moralities of happiness, such as Stoic coldness, Spinoza's end of laughing and weeping, Aristotle's mean, or the letting-go of Hafiz and Goethe, are household remedies for dangerous passions that wrongly speak to everyone.

Every morality is a long tyranny against nature and reason, and this is its value. Poets grew strong under metre and rhyme, and the European mind gained subtlety from centuries of forced interpretation. What matters is "long OBEDIENCE in the same direction." Nature's command, "thou must obey someone for a long time," is addressed to peoples, not individuals. Periodic fasts sharpen impulses: the gloomy English Sunday makes men long for work, the Stoa arose amid Greek sensuality, and Christian pressure sublimated sex into amour-passion.

In the old contest of instinct and reason, instinct has always won in morals. Socrates mocked Athenians who could not justify their acts, found the same in himself, and persuaded reason to back instinct. Plato tried to prove both lead to one good. Descartes, trusting reason alone, was superficial. The doctrine that evil comes from error is plebeian, judging wrongdoing merely stupid, and Plato spent his genius lifting Socrates out of the street.

The slave revolt in morals began with the ancient Jews, whose prophets fused "rich," "violent" and "wicked" and used "poor" for "saint." Herd life has bred a formal conscience that says "Thou shalt" to almost any content, so commanding has grown rare. Modern rulers pretend to be servants of the law or the people. That is why Napoleon came to such people as a deliverance.

Fear is the mother of morals. While enemies threaten, rapacity and love of power are prized. When safety comes, they are branded evil and mediocrity is honoured. Moralists call Cesare Borgia diseased because they hate the tropical. The end is a diseased mellowness in which society sides with the criminal and asks why it should punish at all. "MORALITY IN EUROPE AT PRESENT IS HERDING-ANIMAL MORALITY," one kind beside which higher ones are possible, yet it calls itself morality itself. Democracy is Christianity's political heir, and socialists and anarchists share its hatred of master and servant. All are bound by a religion of sympathy that cannot bear to see suffering and threatens a new Buddhism. Beneath it Nietzsche hears self-contempt, first recorded in Galiani's letter to Madame d'Épinay.

The only hope lies in new philosophers strong enough to transvalue values and steer the future by will instead of chance. The danger is that they fail to appear. Mankind's dwarfing into an equal-rights herd is a real possibility. Chinese mothers teach the proverb "Siao-sin," make thy heart small.


## Our virtues

Actions were first judged by consequences, as in China, where a child's disgrace reflects on its parents. Then they were judged by intention. Nietzsche suspects that the decisive value lies in what is unintended, and that intention-morality must be overcome like alchemy. Modern people live under several moralities at once, like a planet lit by two suns of different colours.

The morality of self-sacrifice pleases everyone involved, which is reason for caution. People differ in what counts as possessing a thing: one man is content with a woman's body, another demands sacrifice, a third must be known entirely. Much helping, educating and ruling is a disguised wish to possess.

Moral condemnation is the favourite revenge of the shallow on the gifted. Those proud of moral discernment never forgive anyone who saw them blunder. Mediocrity resists higher natures with a Jesuitical cunning more interesting than the bourgeois stupidity that obsessed Flaubert.

"Disinterested" action is highly interested. It only looks otherwise because ordinary people cannot see what interests higher natures. The self-sacrificer gave up something here to gain more there. Immoralists are not free of duty. They are woven into a net of finer duties.

A morality of unselfishness for everyone harms higher types, and moral systems must bow before rank. For someone made to command, modesty wastes virtue. Nobility means never lowering one's duties to duties for all, and counting one's privileges among one's duties.

Philosophies that measure worth by pleasure and pain are naive. "The discipline of suffering, of GREAT suffering," produced every elevation of humanity. Man is both clay and sculptor, and the clay must be hammered. Nearly all higher culture rests on spiritualized cruelty: the Roman in the arena, the Spaniard at the bullfight, the Wagnerienne "undergoing" Tristan, and the knower forcing his mind against its own inclination.


## Scholar, sceptic, philosopher

The objective scholar is a precious instrument, a mirror, "no purpose in himself." He can neither affirm nor deny, and says with Leibniz that he despises almost nothing. Science's claim to rule philosophy is a democratic inversion of rank, helped by Schopenhauer's rage against Hegel. Philosophy shrunk to a "theory of knowledge," as with Dühring and Hartmann, is in its death agony. The mass of the sciences tempts the philosopher to specialize or tire. The average scholar, like an old maid granted respectability, practises a "Jesuitism of mediocrity" that relaxes every bent bow. Some truths suit mediocre minds, and Darwin's discoveries may owe something to English narrowness.

Modern scepticism is a sedative, the nervous weakness of a will paralysed by the mixing of classes and races. Will is weakest in France and strongest in Russia. Preferring a sure nothing to an uncertain something is nihilism. There is also a manly German scepticism that "despises and nevertheless grasps," founded by Frederick the Great and saluted in Napoleon's "VOILÀ UN HOMME!" on meeting Goethe.

The coming philosophers will be sterner critics and experimenters, yet critics are only their tools. Kant, "the great Chinaman of Königsberg," was a great critic. Kant and Hegel were philosophical laborers who organized existing values. "THE REAL PHILOSOPHERS, HOWEVER, ARE COMMANDERS AND LAW-GIVERS." Their knowing is creating. They must be new values, and so may even be ignorant. The genuine philosopher lives imprudently, risking himself in many experiments before he judges life. He has always been his age's bad conscience: ironic against tired Athenians, humble against the wilful sixteenth century, hard against our weak-willed one.

Greatness now means range, strength of will and the capacity to stand alone. True thinking feels light and dance-like, as artists know, since necessity and freedom coincide for them. Philosophy cannot be taught, and the right to it comes from descent. Even believing in one's virtues is the grandfathers' old pigtail of good conscience, soon to be cut.


## Nations and Europe

The modern historical sense gives a taste for everything, which makes it ignoble. Moderns enjoy Homer and Shakespeare, whom Voltaire could not stomach, and they itch for the infinite instead of proportion. Lacking a style, the hybrid European borrows historical costumes, and may be original only as a parodist. Morality worn as a public pose now offends refined taste, and that is an advance.

Democratization breeds an adaptable herd type made for servitude, and the same conditions rear tyrants. Nietzsche would welcome a threatening Russia if it forced Europe to one will, for the next century will bring the struggle for world dominion. Mixed inheritance usually yields weak men like Augustine, but sometimes conquerors like Caesar and Leonardo. Europe wishes to be one, and its great men, Napoleon, Goethe, Beethoven and others, worked toward that.

Nationalist follies, anti-Semitism included, are symptoms of a weak national digestion. Nietzsche calls the Jews the strongest race in Europe, says they wish to be accepted, and proposes absorbing them and banishing "the anti-Semitic bawlers." Europe owes them the grand style in morality. Patriotism is a relapse good Europeans recover from quickly, and strength in a statesman is not greatness.

The Germans "HAVE AS YET NO TODAY," as Wagner's Mastersinger overture shows. The German is always becoming, and his honesty is his best disguise. Germans have no ear for prose, which is tempo and sound. In antiquity a period was a single breath, and in Germany only the pulpit kept that art, so Luther's Bible is the best German book. Tempo is hardest to translate, and German lacks the presto of Machiavelli and Petronius.

"Modern ideas" are English, while European nobility of manners is French. France remains Europe's school of taste through its devotion to form, its psychology, and its blend of north and south, as in Bizet. Nietzsche wants a southern music that knows nothing of good and evil. Nations either engender or perfect, and every nation calls its own hypocrisy virtue.

The pages on women date the book most. Nietzsche holds that women exposing "woman as she is" uglifies Europe, that truth is foreign to woman, that dreaming of equal rights shows shallowness, and that emancipation marks declining instinct. Read them as the "my truths" he warned of.


## What is noble

Every elevation of man has come from aristocratic societies with rank and some form of slavery. The outer "pathos of distance" breeds an inner longing to widen distances within the soul. Aristocracies began as barbarian conquerors whose superiority was psychic. A healthy aristocracy sees itself as society's meaning, like the Javanese vine that climbs an oak toward the light, and the French nobility throwing away its privileges was corruption. Life is appropriation and exploitation, so making non-injury society's first principle is a will to deny life.

There are two basic moralities, master and slave, often mixed even in one soul. Master morality is self-glorification. The noble man determines values, sets "good" against "bad," honours hardness and tradition, and owes duties only to equals. Slave morality is a morality of utility. It sets "good" against "evil," prizes sympathy and humility, and its good man drifts toward harmless and slightly stupid. Moral terms were first applied to people and only later to acts. Vanity is a slave's atavism, waiting for others to set its value.

A strong type forms under lasting hardship, as in a Greek polis or Venice. When pressure lifts, individuals and monsters appear together, and moralists can only preach mediocrity in disguise. Egoism belongs to the noble soul, which honours itself in its equals. Easy communication of common needs has pushed mankind toward the average, and education mostly disguises inherited plebeianism.

Nobility shows in self-reverence, not works. "It is not the works, but the BELIEF which is here decisive." An instinct for rank is itself a mark of rank. Different senses of purity divide people most deeply. Noble souls are more easily injured, as a lizard regrows a finger and a man does not. The refined praise only where they disagree, and treat solitude as a virtue.

The ruin of higher men is the rule. Success is the greatest liar, and great men are fictions composed from their works. Genius may be common and its opportunity rare. The greatest thoughts take longest to be understood, like the light of distant stars. Morality is a long falsification that lets the complex soul look simple, and sympathy has value only from a natural master.

Nietzsche would rank philosophers by their laughter, up to golden laughter. He names himself the last initiate of Dionysus, who wants man "stronger, more evil, and more profound." He closes by admitting that his written thoughts are already fading, birds tired from flight that let themselves be caught by hand. The final chapter is the place to start if you read nothing else.
