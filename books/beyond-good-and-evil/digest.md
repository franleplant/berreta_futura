Nietzsche's claim is that philosophy and morality are not what they say they are. They present themselves as the calm pursuit of truth and goodness. Underneath, he argues, they are the work of drives, and above all of the will to power. Europe's reigning morality, which he calls herd morality, takes itself for morality as such, when it is only one kind among many that are possible, some of them higher. The book asks who could stand outside it. His answer is the free spirit, and beyond him a coming philosopher who can bear hard truths, rank values above and below one another, and create new ones beyond the old opposition of good and evil. The book comes in short numbered sections: sharp, uneven, sometimes ugly, often funny. If you read only parts, read the opening chapter on the prejudices of philosophers, the natural history of morals, and the closing chapter on what is noble.

## What philosophers believe without knowing it

Nietzsche opens by treating the great dogmatic systems as a noble childishness. Their real foundations were never what they claimed. Under them lay a popular superstition such as the soul, a trick of grammar, or some narrow personal experience inflated into a universal law. He compares them to astrology, which swallowed vast labour and gold and left behind the grand architecture of Asia and Egypt. The Vedanta and Platonism were awe-inspiring caricatures of the same kind. Dogmatism is something to be outgrown.

Kant is his first test case. Kant asked how synthetic judgments a priori are possible and answered that they are possible by means of a faculty. Nietzsche says this only repeats the question, like Molière's doctor who explains that opium puts people to sleep because it has a "virtus dormitiva". He pictures the Tübingen theologians rushing into the groves to hunt for faculties. His own proposal is that "it is high time to replace the Kantian question, "How are synthetic judgments a PRIORI possible?" by another question, "Why is belief in such judgments necessary?"" Such judgments may well be false. Creatures like us may still need to believe them in order to survive.

He thinks grammar does much of the steering. Philosophers who imagine themselves independent keep circling the same orbit, and their ideas belong together like the animals of one continent. Indian, Greek and German philosophy look alike because their languages share grammatical functions. Where the concept of the subject is least developed, as in the Ural-Altaic languages, he expects a different reading of the world. Philosophizing, on this view, is less discovery than remembering.

The worst error so far, he says, was Plato's invention of Pure Spirit and the Good in Itself. To speak of these as absolutes outside any viewpoint denies perspective, which is the basic condition of life. He asks, as a physician would, how such a sickness attacked the finest product of antiquity, and whether Socrates corrupted Plato. Christianity is Platonism for the people. The long fight against both has strung the European spirit like a bow, and with a bow so taut one can aim at the furthest goals. Jesuitism and democratic enlightenment, helped along by the press, were two attempts to unbend it. The free spirits, the good Europeans, keep the tension, and perhaps the arrow and the target too.

Then he turns on truth itself. Voltaire said one seeks truth only to do good, and Nietzsche bets that whoever goes about it so humanely finds nothing. Even "knowledge for its own sake" is a moral ideal, the last snare morality lays. The deeper question is why we want truth at all, and why not untruth, or uncertainty, or ignorance. He compares it to Oedipus before the Sphinx: it is unclear who is questioning whom. "It is nothing more than a moral prejudice that truth is worth more than semblance; it is, in fact, the worst proved supposition in the world." In place of a hard line between true and false, he suggests degrees of seeming, the way painters speak of valeurs, lighter and darker tones. He suspects that faith in grammar's subject and predicate props up the old opposition.

Behind it lies the metaphysicians' basic faith. "The fundamental belief of metaphysicians is THE BELIEF IN ANTITHESES OF VALUES." They assume truth cannot come from error or the generous act from selfishness, so they give high things a separate origin in the lap of Being or the Thing-in-itself. Nietzsche calls these popular oppositions provisional frog perspectives, seen from below. Perhaps good things are knotted to the evil things they seem to oppose, perhaps even identical with them. Language misleads here, speaking of opposites where there are only degrees. The supposed miracle of the saint, a sudden swing from bad to good, is a misreading of the facts by a psychology that believed in moral opposites. Schopenhauer's question of how the saint is possible, Wagner's Kundry and the Salvation Army all belong here. Even what an age calls evil is usually an untimely echo of what an earlier age called good.

Most thinking, he goes on, is instinct. Behind logic stand physiological demands, valuations such as the certain being worth more than the uncertain, which may be only the conditions for preserving creatures like us. Philosophers are advocates who will not admit it: the wish comes first, the arguments after. Kant's Tartuffery leads to the categorical imperative. Spinoza's mathematical form is mail and mask over the timidity of a sickly recluse. Even philosophical quarrels have low motives. Epicurus mocked the Platonists as actors because he envied Plato's grand staging, while he himself sat in his little garden writing three hundred books. At some point in every system the philosopher's conviction walks on, like the ass in the old mystery play, hailed as beautiful and very strong.

So every great philosophy is the confession and involuntary autobiography of its author, and its moral aim is the seed from which the rest grows. To understand any metaphysics, ask what morality it is after. Each drive wants to rule and would gladly call itself the end of existence. The Stoics preached living according to Nature, but Nature is boundlessly wasteful, indifferent, without purpose or justice. "To live--is not that just endeavouring to be otherwise than this Nature?" asks Nietzsche, and he answers that living means valuing, preferring and being unjust. The Stoics did not read their law in Nature. They dictated it. Every philosophy that believes in itself does the same, and philosophy is the most spiritual form of will to power.

He ends the chapter with two diagnoses. Those who prefer a sure nothing to an uncertain something are nihilists, despairing and worn out, however brave they look. The modern skeptics who disparage appearance and their own bodies are driven by a sound disgust with the motley of positivism and its "reality-philosophasters". What matters is that they want to get away. With more strength they would go forward instead of back. Natural science, finally, arranges and interprets the world; it does not explain it. It persuades a plebeian age because it trusts what can be seen and touched, where Plato threw cold grey nets of concepts over the mob of the senses. Physicists read "conformity to law" into Nature from a democratic taste for equality before the law. Someone else could read the same facts as the absence of all law, with every power drawing its consequences at every moment. Nietzsche admits this is interpretation too, and he welcomes the objection. This opening chapter is the part most worth reading whole.


## The soul, the will and the will to power

Boscovich, like Copernicus, beat the evidence of the senses by dissolving belief in matter and the solid atom. Nietzsche wants the same done to soul-atomism, the Christian belief in the soul as an eternal, indivisible unit. He does not want the soul thrown out with it. The naturalists lose the soul the moment they touch it. He proposes better hypotheses: the mortal soul, the soul as a multiplicity, the soul as a social structure of drives and passions. The new psychologist who works on these is condemned to invent, and perhaps to discover.

Descartes' "I think" gets the same treatment. It assumes that there is an I, that something must do the thinking, that thinking is an activity caused by an ego, and that one already knows what thinking is. Naming a state "thinking" means comparing it with other states, so the knowledge cannot be immediate. Immediate certainty is a contradiction in terms. In fact a thought comes when "it" wishes, and not when "I" wish. The ego behind the thought is an inference from grammar, which demands a doer for every deed. Atomism learned to do without the little lump behind force, and we may learn to do without the little "one".

Willing is not simple either, though Schopenhauer took it to be the one thing we know completely. It is a bundle of sensations, a ruling thought, and above all an emotion of command. The feeling of free will comes from a double role. The one who wills both commands and obeys, then identifies with the part that commands and takes credit when the order is carried out. Nietzsche compares it to a well-run commonwealth whose ruling class claims every success: "L'effet c'est moi." The body is a society of many under-souls, so willing belongs to the doctrine of relations of rule. Even overcoming an emotion is the work of other emotions.

The theory of free will survives, he jokes, because it is so refutable that every clever newcomer wants a go at it. In its metaphysical form it demands that one be causa sui, one's own cause, like Munchausen pulling himself out of the swamp by his own hair. That is pride. But the opposite doctrine is no better. Cause, law, number and purpose are useful fictions for communication, and treating them as mechanical pushing in things themselves is mythology. "The "non-free will" is mythology; in real life it is only a question of STRONG and WEAK wills." How a person takes the question shows their character: the vain cling to their merits, and the self-contemptuous want to be answerable for nothing, sometimes dressing this up as sympathy with criminals.

From here he states his central hypothesis. Self-preservation is not life's main instinct. "A living thing seeks above all to DISCHARGE its strength," and survival is only a frequent result. Method forbids positing many kinds of cause until one has been pushed to its limit. If our world of drives is the only thing given, we should try to read all active force, mechanical as well as organic, as will to power. Psychology, crippled so far by moral squeamishness, should become the morphology and theory of development of that will. The inquiry is dangerous and may wreck one's own remaining morals; he pictures it as a sea voyage straight over morality, teeth set, hand on the helm. Those who make it may say psychology is once again queen of the sciences.


## Why we need untruth, and why the deep wear masks

"The falseness of an opinion is not for us any objection to it: it is here, perhaps, that our new language sounds most strangely." The question is whether a judgment furthers life. The falsest ones, such as synthetic a priori judgments, logical fictions and the counterfeiting of the world by numbers, may be the ones we can least do without. A philosophy that admits this already stands beyond good and evil.

Knowledge, he argues, was built on a stronger will to ignorance. We made the world simple and clear by deciding not to know most of it, and the will to knowledge is a refinement of that will, not its enemy. Perception itself is mostly invention. Germans turned the Latin arcubalista into Armbrust. Readers guess most words on a page. Nietzsche admits he imagines the fine expressions on the face of the person he is talking to. We have been used to lying since remote ages. The spirit works like a stomach with limited digestion: it assimilates the new to the old, simplifies, shuts out what it cannot take, and enjoys masks. Morality belongs here too. Complex, artful man invented the good conscience so he could enjoy his soul as something simple, which ties morality closely to art.

None of this makes truth a matter of comfort. Rigorous spirits will smile "when any one says in their presence "That thought elevates me, why should it not be true?"" Happiness and virtue prove nothing. A truth might be harmful, and full knowledge of existence might destroy us. The strength of a mind can be measured by how much truth it can bear without having it sweetened or veiled. Stendhal said a good philosopher should be dry and clear, without illusion, like a banker who has made his fortune.

Hence masks. There is a rank between the esoteric view from above and the exoteric view from below, and the deepest insights must sound like folly or crime to those not suited to them. Books for everyone smell of small people. Everything profound loves a mask. A man with something precious and fragile to hide might roll through life like an old green wine cask heavy with hoops. A mask also grows around a deep spirit by itself, from the shallow way others read every word. Every philosophy hides another behind it, and every cave has a deeper cave. A finer humanity respects masks. A deep thinker fears being understood more than being misunderstood, because misunderstanding wounds only his vanity.

The aphorisms in this stretch are among the best in the book and deserve reading in the original. Youth loves and despises without condition, and the art of nuance is life's best gain. Memory says "I did that," pride says "I could not have done that," and memory yields. Reason and conscience both bow to the strongest impulse. Irony and distrust are signs of health, and everything absolute is pathological. Madness is rare in individuals and the rule in parties, nations and epochs. "One loves ultimately one's desires, not the thing desired." People differ less in what they want than in what they count as having it, so that charity, parenting, teaching and rule turn out to be forms of possession.


## What religion does to people

Early Christian faith, for Nietzsche, was a sacrifice of freedom, pride and the self-confidence of the spirit, a long suicide of reason in refined minds, with Pascal as the model. "God on the Cross" promised to turn the ancient values upside down. It was the slave's revenge against noble Rome, which had smiled at faith and refused to make much of suffering. He names the French Revolution as the last great slave revolt.

Taste in religion shows rank. Greek religion poured out gratitude, which marks a superior kind of man; fear came only when the populace gained ground. The Old Testament is great, the New is petty, and liking the Old is a touchstone of great and small. Binding them into one Bible was an enormous literary daring. The powerful bowed to the saint because they sensed the will to power in his self-mastery, and suspected his privations hid some knowledge of a great danger.

Modern philosophy since Descartes has been quietly anti-Christian, because it attacks the soul, which was believed in the way one believed in the grammatical subject. It is not anti-religious. Kant even suspected the subject might be mere appearance, close to the Vedanta. Meanwhile modern busyness breeds unbelief better than any argument, since religion needs idleness with a good conscience. The laborious scholar who looks down on the religious man is being naive.

Religious cruelty climbs a ladder. First people sacrificed those they loved, as in primitive firstling offerings or Tiberius in the Mithra grotto on Capri. Then, in the moral age, they sacrificed their own strongest instincts. Last they sacrificed God himself and worshipped stone, fate, nothingness. That last step belongs to the coming generation. But whoever looks to the bottom of pessimism, past the moral spell that held Buddha and Schopenhauer, may find its opposite: a man so in love with the world that he wants it all again exactly as it was, calling da capo for eternity. Human shallowness, meanwhile, is a preservative instinct. Whole centuries clung to religion from fear of seeing too much too soon, and piety is the most refined product of the fear of truth.

The philosopher, Nietzsche says, will use religion as a tool of discipline. It binds subjects to rulers, shields contemplative nobles from politics, trains rising classes in self-control, and gives the many contentment with their lot. The Brahmins nominated kings while standing above them. When religion rules as an end, though, it sides with the failures, who are the majority, and keeps alive too much that should have perished. Christianity and Buddhism broke strong worldly instincts and bred a dwarfed, sickly herd European. An artist with a divine hammer, he writes, would cry out at the bunglers who ruined his finest stone.


## A natural history of morals

This chapter is the core of the book. "There is no such thing as moral phenomena, but only a moral interpretation of phenomena." Philosophers who set out to give morality a foundation never questioned morality itself. Knowing only their own time and place, they offered a learned form of faith in what already prevailed. Schopenhauer named "hurt no one, help everyone you can" as the basis of ethics, and this self-declared pessimist then played the flute every day after dinner. What is needed is humble collecting and comparing toward a typology of moralities. Each moral system says something about its author: it justifies, soothes, avenges or conceals. Kant's says, in effect, what is admirable in me is that I can obey, and it shall be no different with you. "In short, systems of morals are only a SIGN-LANGUAGE OF THE EMOTIONS."

Every morality is a long tyranny against nature and reason, and Nietzsche says this is no objection. Everything free and masterly grew from long obedience to arbitrary rules. Poets found strength inside meter and rhyme. European thinkers gained their subtlety from centuries spent inside church and Aristotle. Nature's command reads: obey someone, and for a long time. Moral fanaticism works like a fast in which drives learn to hunger and sharpen, which is how Christian restraint turned sexuality into romantic love. The English Sunday makes people long for Monday.

Socrates equated evil with error, a plebeian, utilitarian idea that sees only the bad consequences of wrongdoing. Plato was too noble for it and spent his genius remaking Socrates in his own image. Socrates saw that moral judgments rest on instinct, mocked the Athenian nobles who could not explain themselves, found the same failing in himself, and then talked himself into supplying reasons for his instincts. Every philosopher and theologian after him followed, so in morality instinct, or "faith", or the herd, has won.

The slave revolt in morals began, he says, with the Jewish prophets, who fused "rich" and "powerful" with "wicked", made "poor" mean "holy", and turned "world" into a reproach. Europe also owes them the grand style in morality, the majesty of infinite demands, which is both the best and worst of its inheritance.

Fear is the mother of morals. Boldness, vengefulness and love of power were needed while enemies threatened. Once a community is safe, whatever lifts a person above the herd is called evil, and mediocrity is honored. In Rome a sympathetic act was neither good nor bad. Later societies begin to side with the criminal and ask why they should punish at all. Moralists call Cesare Borgia diseased, when he was the healthiest of tropical monsters, because they hate everything that is not temperate.

Obedience has been bred longest, so a formal conscience now obeys whoever shouts "thou shalt". Commanders must pretend to be servants of the people or the law, which Nietzsche calls the moral hypocrisy of the commanding class. That is why Napoleon felt like a deliverance. Morality in Europe today is herd morality, and it claims to be morality itself, though other and higher moralities are possible. Democracy inherits Christianity, and socialists and anarchists share its hatred of privilege and its religion of sympathy. A selfless morality forced on everyone harms the higher type, and it is immoral, he says, to claim that what is right for one is right for another.

Morality first judged acts by consequences, as in China, where a child's success or disgrace fell on the parents. Then it judged by intention. Nietzsche suspects the real value lies in what is unintended, intention being only the skin of an act, and that intention-morality belongs with astrology and alchemy. Self-sacrifice wants something back. Moralities of happiness, from Stoic coldness to Aristotle's mean, are prudential recipes against one's own passions, "expediency, expediency, expediency, mixed with stupidity, stupidity, stupidity." The English utilitarians, Bentham walking in Helvetius' footsteps, dress up English comfort as "general welfare". The sweetness of feelings "for others" is a reason for suspicion. The chapter closes on two famous lines. "He who fights with monsters should be careful lest he thereby become a monster. And if thou gaze long into an abyss, the abyss will also gaze into thee." And: "What is done out of love always takes place beyond good and evil."


## Free spirits and the philosophers to come

Nietzsche waits for a new kind of philosopher, the philosopher of the dangerous Perhaps, with tastes opposite to those that have ruled so far. These will be tempters, not dogmatists. They will love truth but not want everyone to share it: "My opinion is MY opinion: another person has not easily a right to it." Whatever can be common has little value, so a "common good" contradicts itself. Europe's so-called free thinkers are something else, levellers who blame old institutions for suffering and want universal comfort. The real free spirits hold that the plant called man has grown strongest under danger, compulsion and hardship, and that everything terrible in man serves his elevation as much as its opposite.

Independence is for the very few, "a privilege of the strong." Whoever chooses it without need enters a labyrinth where some minotaur of conscience may tear him apart beyond anyone's sympathy. He must test himself by clinging to nothing: no person, however dear, no fatherland, no science, not even his own liberation or virtues. The best test is knowing how to conserve oneself.

He warns philosophers against martyrdom. Suffering for truth turns the thinker into an agitator and actor, closed to objection. Spinoza and Giordano Bruno, forced into solitude, became refined poisoners under their masks. Better to keep concealment and question marks. Moral indignation is a sign that a thinker has lost his humour, and the indignant man is the greatest liar; the laughing satyr teaches more. Morality worn as a public pose now offends taste, and that is progress. People proud of their moral subtlety never forgive anyone who saw them err. Psychologists should study how mediocrity's instinctive cunning works against the exception, which proves instinct to be the most intelligent kind of intelligence. Moral condemnation is the favourite revenge of the shallow on the gifted, and it is why they need "equality before God".

Science has declared independence from philosophy, and Nietzsche reads this as democracy's "no masters" carried into the mind. Young scholars picked up one philosopher's contempt for the rest, as Schopenhauer's rage against Hegel cut a generation off from German culture. The modern philosophers themselves, Dühring and von Hartmann among them, gave reason enough for scorn. Philosophy shrunk to a theory of knowledge cannot rule. The sheer size of the sciences threatens the young philosopher, who may tire, specialize, or reach the summit too late.

The scholar may have a real impersonal drive to know, a small clockwork that could as well serve mushrooms as philology. He is industrious, envious, and mediocre, and his instinct works to slacken every tense bow. The objective man is a precious mirror, "PRESQUE RIEN", an instrument for a stronger hand. Modern skepticism, often sold as objectivity or art for art's sake, is paralysis of will, which Nietzsche traces to the mixing of long-separated classes and races. It is strongest in old France and weakest in Russia. Against it stands a manly skepticism that "despises and nevertheless grasps", seen in Frederick the Great and in the man Napoleon greeted in Goethe.

Critics and philosophical workers such as Kant and Hegel order existing values. Real philosophers command and legislate: "Their "knowing" is CREATING, their creating is a law-giving." Against democracy's dwarfing of man, hope lies in such minds. The genuine philosopher lives imprudently and risks himself to judge the worth of life. His thinking joins exuberance with rigor, as artists feel freest when acting from necessity. His right to the highest problems comes from generations of breeding. He has always been the bad conscience of his age, cutting into its proudest virtues, as Socrates' irony cut into the tired Athenian nobles. Today greatness means breadth, will and solitude: how much one can bear and answer for.


## The virtues of free spirits, and their limits

If free spirits have virtues, they will be the ones that suit their secret inclinations, and even looking for them is the grandfathers' good conscience surviving, an old pigtail worn behind the head. Fools call the immoralists men without duty, but they are bound by a fine net of duties. Honesty is their last virtue. They should keep it hard, calling in curiosity, the love of the forbidden and the will to power when it tires, and they should watch that it does not become an ornament. Every virtue drifts toward stupidity; the Russians say "stupid to the point of sanctity".

Modern people live under several moralities at once, like a planet lit by two suns of different colours, so their acts seldom mean one thing. Mixed instincts usually make people weak and hungry for peace, as in Augustine. Sometimes the inner war becomes power, and the result is Alcibiades, Caesar, Frederick II, Leonardo. The hybrid European treats history as a costume shop, trying on romantic, classical, Christian and national styles in turn, and his one future may be a grand carnival of laughter. His historical sense, a taste for everything, is ignoble. It lets him enjoy Homer and Shakespeare, but noble cultures had a firm yes and no, and the perfected moments of culture are the hardest for him to feel.

Hedonism, pessimism and utilitarianism all judge by pleasure and pain, which Nietzsche calls naive. Great suffering has bred every elevation of man. In man creature and creator are joined: clay and mire, but also the sculptor and the hammer. Ordinary pity spares the clay what it needs. His own sympathy goes the other way. The modern preaching of pity hides self-contempt, and sympathy is worth something only from a natural master who can guard and punish. Against Europe's tender complaining he prescribes the gay science.

Nearly all higher culture rests on cruelty made spiritual: the Roman at the arena, the Christian before the cross, the Spaniard at the bullfight, the Wagnerian lady at "Tristan". The wild beast was transfigured, not killed. Cruelty also turns inward, in asceticism and in knowledge, which forces the spirit to say no where it would like to adore. The free spirit's task is to translate man back into nature, to read the terrible original text "homo natura" with fearless Oedipus eyes and ears stopped like Ulysses' against the metaphysicians.

Then come his notorious pages on women. He begins by granting that every thinker has at bottom something unteachable, a granite of spiritual fate, so what follows are only "my truths". He calls woman's effort to explain woman scientifically ugly, says her great art is appearance and not truth, and approves of Napoleon silencing Madame de Staël. To deny the basic antagonism of the sexes and dream of equal rights is, for him, a sign of shallowness; he notes the Greeks grew stricter with women as they grew in power. He holds that emancipation has lowered woman's influence since the Revolution, and in passing calls slavery a condition of every higher culture. These pages are of interest mainly as evidence of the book's limits.


## Nations, music and the future of Europe

Hearing Wagner's Mastersinger overture, heavy, pompous, without grace or South, Nietzsche says of the Germans that they belong to the day before yesterday and the day after tomorrow: "THEY HAVE AS YET NO TODAY." Their soul is a heap of many souls from a great mixing of races, so the German does not exist but is always becoming, and "development" is the German idea, in Hegel and in Wagner. German depth is often slow digestion. German honesty is the most successful disguise of all; the German looks at foreigners with faithful, blue, empty eyes, and they mistake him for his dressing gown.

Patriotism is an atavism the good European passes through in hours and duller races in generations. He gives his own warm words on Wagner as a relapse of that kind. Two deaf old patriots argue over a statesman who forced an unpolitical people into high politics: one calls him great, the other shouts that he is strong and mad, not great. A people in national fever has fits of stupidity, and Nietzsche lists the German ones: anti-French, anti-Semitic, anti-Polish, Christian-romantic, Wagnerian, Teutonic, Prussian.

Some nations, like the Greeks and French, receive and form. Others, like the Jews, the Romans and perhaps the Germans, fertilize and create. Every nation calls its hypocrisy its virtue. The English are unphilosophical, from Bacon to Hume and Locke, and cling to Christianity to moralize a coarse nature, with Methodism teaching drunkards "moral grunting". Some truths are best found by narrow, careful minds like Darwin, Mill and Spencer, and the creator of values may need to be ignorant. Modern ideas are English, and the French were only their victims; European nobility of taste is French. That France of taste still exists, hidden behind the vulgar foreground of Victor Hugo's funeral, in Stendhal and in Bizet's piece of the South in music.

Democracy, beneath its talk of progress, is a physiological levelling. It produces a nomadic, super-national European whose mark is adaptability. It breeds a mass of talkative, weak-willed, handy workers who need a master, and at the same time it gives the exception more schooling and disguise than ever, so it rears tyrants in every sense. The Jews he calls the toughest people in Europe. They could dominate and want only to settle and be respected, so Europe should meet them halfway and expel the anti-Semitic bawlers; he even imagines their genius for money and patience joined to the Prussian officer's art of command. Despite the nationalist craze Europe wants to be one, as Napoleon, Goethe, Beethoven, Stendhal, Heine, Schopenhauer and Wagner already knew, becoming patriots only when resting from themselves. His serious topic is a new ruling caste for Europe with one long will, and he would welcome a Russian threat that forced it.

On style he is at his liveliest. Tempo is the hardest thing to translate, and German cannot run presto, so it cannot carry Aristophanes or Machiavelli. Plato died with Aristophanes under his pillow. Good prose needs a third ear for rhythm and vowels, which German readers lack. The ancients read aloud, and a period was one breath. In Germany only the preacher knew the weight of a syllable, so "the BIBLE has hitherto been the best German book." German music lost its European voice after Mozart and Beethoven, sinking through Schumann into a national affair. Wagner is kin to the French Romantics, Delacroix nearest of all, and like them he ended bowing to Rome in Parsifal. Against all this Nietzsche dreams of a Southern music "that knew nothing more of good and evil".


## What makes a person noble

The last chapter gathers the book. "EVERY elevation of the type "man," has hitherto been the work of an aristocratic society and so it will always be." Outward distance between ranks first taught the soul an inward longing for distance, the self-overcoming of man. Noble castes began as barbarians whose advantage was wholeness of will, falling on peaceful traders or tired old cultures. A healthy aristocracy sees itself as the meaning of society, not its servant, and accepts the sacrifice of many in good conscience. The French nobles who threw away their privileges were finishing a long decay. Nietzsche's image is the Javanese vine that climbs an oak to spread its crown in the light.

Life is appropriation, injury, conquest and exploitation. Non-injury works among equals inside one body, but made the principle of society it becomes a will to deny life. Hoping for a society without exploitation is like hoping for life without organic functions.

There are two basic moralities, master and slave, often mixed even in one soul. Value words first named kinds of men and only later actions. In master morality "good" and "bad" mean noble and contemptible; the Greek nobles called themselves "we truthful ones". "Good" and "evil" come from slave morality. The noble man creates values and needs no approval. He reveres hardness, as in the saga line "Wotan placed a hard heart in my breast", and helps the unfortunate from overflowing power, not pity. He owes duties only to equals. He reveres age and tradition, where faith in progress betrays an ignoble origin. Egoism, the belief that others are naturally subject to "us", belongs to his soul, and among equals it becomes delicate reciprocity, as each star is an egoist in its course. Slave morality is a morality of utility. It fears the powerful, honours patience, humility and the warm heart, and its "good man" drifts toward the harmless fool, the French bonhomme. The longing for freedom belongs to it, while reverence belongs to nobles, which is why passionate love was invented by the Provençal knights. Vanity is a slave's habit of accepting the value others assign.

A strong type forms in long struggle with hard, constant conditions, as in a Greek polis or Venice. When the pressure lifts, the old discipline breaks, growth and decay burst out together, and moralists preach mediocrity disguised as moderation. Late civilizations teach "Siao-sin", make thy heart small. Ease of communication has been the strongest force on mankind, pulling it toward the common, since shared words need shared experiences and rare souls stay alone.

An instinct for rank and reverence is itself a mark of high rank, and peasants may show more of it than the cultured. What ancestors did cannot be educated out. A different sense of purity separates people most. Higher people usually perish; the psychologist who sees this needs cheerfulness to survive it. Great men are fictions composed after the work, and behind Byron, Poe or Kleist lie damaged souls. Love more often destroys than saves, and Nietzsche reads the life of Jesus this way. How deeply one can suffer nearly fixes one's rank. Genius may not be rare, only the luck to meet the right moment, so the Raphael without hands may be the rule. The finer soul is more easily wounded; a lizard regrows a finger and a man does not.

The noble do not lower their duties to everyone's. Whoever strives for great things sees others as means, hindrance or rest, and lives in poisoned solitude until he arrives. The ideal is proud calm, riding one's passions like horses and keeping four virtues: courage, insight, sympathy and solitude. One learns what one needed only after building the house. The greatest thoughts take longest to reach us, like light from distant stars. Writing catches thoughts only as they tire: "We immortalize what cannot live and fly much longer, things only which are exhausted and mellow!" Nobility shows not in works but in the noble soul's reverence for itself.

He ends by ranking philosophers by their laughter, up to golden laughter, against Hobbes. Then Dionysus appears, the tempter god, with Ariadne beside him, and Nietzsche calls himself his last disciple. The god says: "I like man, and often think how I can still further advance him, and make him stronger, more evil, and more profound." This chapter, more than any other, repays reading whole.
