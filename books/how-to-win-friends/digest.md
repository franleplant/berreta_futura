Getting on with people does more for your income and your happiness than technical skill, and it is a set of habits anyone can learn. Stop criticizing, because criticism only makes people defend themselves and resent you. Feed the hunger everyone has to feel important by giving honest, specific appreciation. Take a real interest in others: listen, use their names, and talk about what matters to them. When you want someone to change their mind or behavior, avoid arguments, let them save face, and make them want what you propose. None of it works as a trick. It has to be sincere, and it is needed most at home.

## Why this skill is worth learning

Speaking in public is a short cut to distinction and influence. A person who talks well is lifted above the crowd and is often credited with more ability than they have, and leadership tends to drift toward them. Godfrey Meyer, a banker who was struck dumb in a first speaking class, later spoke at a town meeting about a tax bill and was elected to the town council at the head of ninety-six candidates.

Confidence comes from doing the thing you fear until you have a record of successes behind you. If you speak at every session in front of a sympathetic audience, the fear slowly turns into courage, and that courage carries into business and private life. The speaking is only the means. The real work is conquering fear.

Eloquence comes from conviction more than from talent. Almost anyone can speak acceptably if they have self-confidence and an idea boiling inside them. Knock the most ignorant person in town down, and they will get up and talk with the eloquence of William Jennings Bryan.

**Even in technical fields, about 15 percent of financial success comes from technical knowledge and about 85 percent from skill in dealing with people.** That is the finding of research cited from the Carnegie Foundation. Technical skill is cheap and plentiful; knowledge joined to the ability to express ideas and arouse enthusiasm is what commands the highest pay. Rockefeller said he would pay more for the ability to deal with people than for any other ability under the sun.

Adults know they need this. A survey of every adult in Meriden, Connecticut, found that after health, their chief interest was people: how to understand them, how to be liked, how to win them over. Schools teach Latin and mathematics and nothing on this subject, and no practical handbook existed. The rules that follow were not worked out in theory; thousands of adults applied them at work and at home over fifteen years and reported what happened. One employer of 314 people, who had always criticized them, switched to these methods and found that the workers became friends who greeted him by his first name.

Most of us use only a small part of our abilities. William James held that we are only half awake, living far inside our limits. The aim here is to wake the dormant part.

The point of all this is action, not knowledge. Judge it by one test: whether you handle real situations a little better afterwards. If you don't, it has failed.

To get anything out of it:

1. **Want it badly.** No study method matters as much as a deep, driving desire to deal better with people. Keep that desire alive by reminding yourself how much your popularity, happiness and income depend on it.
2. **Reread often.** We forget astonishingly fast, so read each part twice and review the whole every month. An insurance office manager rereads every contract monthly for the same reason.
3. **Practice until it becomes habit.** Criticizing and talking about your own wants come naturally, so applying the principles is hard. Pause before you act on impulse, and use these ideas as a working handbook.
4. **Review your week.** Every week, go over your dealings with people and ask what mistakes you made, what you did well and what you learned. A Wall Street bank president with little schooling did this every Saturday evening and credited most of his success to it.

> Only knowledge that is used sticks in your mind.


## Why criticism fails, and what works instead

People almost never blame themselves for anything, however wrong they are. Even hardened criminals rationalize what they did and see themselves as decent or wronged. "Two Gun" Crowley, who shot a policeman, wrote that he had a kind heart that would do nobody harm. If killers think this way, the ordinary people you deal with will too, and any approach that depends on them condemning themselves will fail.

That is why criticism is futile. It does not produce correction; it produces self-justification, and the criticized person turns the blame back on you. When Theodore Roosevelt denounced Taft, Taft only said, close to tears, that he could not see how he could have acted differently.

Criticism is also dangerous. It wounds pride, and the resentment can last a lifetime. It can end careers: criticism led Thomas Hardy to give up writing fiction, and it drove Chatterton to suicide.

> When dealing with people, let us remember we are not dealing with creatures of logic. We are dealing with creatures of emotion

So before you rebuke anyone, picture yourself in their place, let your anger cool, and hold back. After Meade let Lee escape, Lincoln wrote Meade a severe letter and never sent it; it was found among Lincoln's papers after Lincoln's death. Lincoln had learned that people act as we would act in their circumstances. The German army had a rule along these lines: a soldier with a grievance had to sleep on it for a night before filing a complaint.

Work on yourself before you try to improve others. Improving yourself pays better and carries far less risk. And instead of condemning people, try to understand why they act as they do. Criticism takes no skill; understanding and forgiveness take character and self-control, and they breed sympathy and kindness. Benjamin Franklin was tactless as a young man, resolved to speak ill of no one, and became diplomatic enough to be sent as ambassador to France.

**The only way to get anyone to do anything is to make them want to do it.** Force and threats get compliance only until your back is turned, and they leave bad consequences behind. A revolver can make someone hand over a watch, and that is about all it can do. Every act a person performs comes from wanting something. Even a gift to the Red Cross is made because the giver wanted the feeling of a helpful act more than the money.

The deepest and least satisfied of those wants is the desire to feel important. Food and sleep usually get met; the craving for importance rarely does. John Dewey called it the deepest urge in human nature. A farmer may proudly hang the blue ribbons won by prize hogs on a sheet, though the hogs care nothing for them.

The craving is strong enough that some people seem to retreat into illness, or even insanity, to satisfy it. Some people turn invalid to win attention; Mrs. McKinley made the President neglect affairs of state to soothe her. Meanwhile the rest of us starve each other of appreciation. We would think it criminal to leave family or employees without food for days, yet we leave them without a kind word for years. The actor Alfred Lunt said there was nothing he needed so much as nourishment for his self-esteem.

This urge is not all bad. It drives civilization: it led Lincoln, a poor grocery clerk, to study law books found in a barrel. And how a person chooses to satisfy it shows their character. Rockefeller got a sense of importance by funding a hospital in Peking; Dillinger got one by robbing banks and announcing his name.

Because the hunger is so intense, whoever honestly feeds it gains remarkable influence. People who will go mad to feel important will respond to honest appreciation given in ordinary life. It has to be honest. That condition comes up again and again.

You develop the best in people through appreciation and encouragement, while criticism from superiors kills ambition. Charles Schwab was paid a million dollars a year largely for the ability to arouse enthusiasm, and did it by praising lavishly and refusing to criticize. Most people do the opposite: they complain about what they dislike and say nothing about what they like. Even when someone fails, look for something real to praise. When Edward Bedford lost a million dollars on a South American venture, Rockefeller congratulated Bedford on saving sixty percent of the investment.

Appreciation is not flattery. Appreciation is sincere and unselfish; flattery is insincere, selfish, cheap praise, often just telling people what they already think of themselves. Flattery may work on people starved for praise, and a rare genius like Disraeli could spread it on with a trowel for Queen Victoria, but with discerning people it seldom works, and in the long run it does more harm than good. Ordinary people should not count on it.

To find honest things to praise, stop thinking about yourself. We spend about 95 percent of our idle thought on ourselves. Turn that attention to other people's good points and you will never need flattery. Emerson held that every person he met was in some way his superior and had something to teach him.

None of this works as a technique. Used mechanically, as a trick, it fails, because people crave appreciation and also detect insincerity. It is a way of living, not a bag of tricks.

The way to influence people is to talk about what they want and show them how to get it. Everyone is endlessly interested in their own wants and hardly at all in yours. Bait the hook to suit the fish. The Emersons could not push a calf into the barn, but the housemaid led it in by letting it suck her finger.

The same applies to letters and sales. Letters that open with the sender's wants and problems irritate readers who are busy with their own; a freight terminal's letter that described its own congestion and mentioned the customer's benefit only at the end produced antagonism, not cooperation. People do not want to be sold. They want their problems solved, and they like to feel that they are buying. A throat specialist who asked about a patient's income before looking at the throat lost that patient's trust at once.

So the first step in persuading anyone is to arouse in them an eager want. Before you ask someone to act, ask how you can make them want to act. Andrew Carnegie got neglectful nephews to answer by return mail by mentioning in a postscript that money was enclosed, and leaving it out. Storming in with your own demands does the opposite: it starts an argument, and even if you prove the other side wrong, their pride makes it hard for them to back down.

Success depends on seeing things from the other person's point of view as well as your own. Henry Ford said as much, and most people ignore it most of the time. A tenant faced with a 300 percent rent increase from a hotel wrote down the manager's advantages and disadvantages from the manager's side; the increase was cut to 50 percent. Because most people are grabbing for themselves, the rare person who serves others unselfishly faces little competition.


## How to make people like you

**People are interested in themselves, not in you, so the way to be liked is to be genuinely interested in them.** A New York Telephone Company study found that "I" was the word used most often in phone calls, and in a group photograph the face you look for first is your own. Each of us lives at the center of a small private map. The rules for being liked all start from that fact.

1. **Become genuinely interested in other people.** You will make more friends in two months this way than in two years of trying to get people interested in you. Theodore Roosevelt greeted every White House servant by name.

   Alfred Adler held that the person who takes no interest in others has the hardest time in life and does the most harm; every human failure, in his view, comes from such people. Real interest can win the time of even the busiest and most important people: a banker got information that a reluctant executive would not give by bringing stamps for the executive's son. Show interest with effort, by doing things that cost time and thought: the Prince of Wales studied Spanish for months before touring South America. Greet people with animation and enthusiasm, even on the telephone.

   Interest in someone's problems wins them over faster than any pitch. C. M. Knaphle, a coal salesman, had failed for years to sell to a chain-store executive. When Knaphle asked the executive for facts to defend chain stores in a debate, the executive talked for nearly two hours and then offered to buy coal without being asked.

2. **Smile.** A sincere smile says that you like the person and are glad to see them, and it counts for more than clothes. It has to be real; a mechanical grin is detected and resented. The stockbroker William B. Steinhardt started smiling at a spouse, the doorman and colleagues for a week, and found it changed the marriage, the handling of complaints and the earnings.

   You also have to enjoy your work and your meetings with people, because others enjoy meeting you only if you enjoy meeting them. A rubber-company chairman noticed that men succeeded while the business was fun and failed once it became mere work. If you don't feel like smiling, act as if you do. William James argued that action is under more direct control of the will than feeling is, so by behaving cheerfully you can bring on cheerfulness. Happiness depends on what you think about your circumstances, not on the circumstances themselves.

3. **Remember people's names.** A person cares more about their own name than about all other names on earth put together, so using it easily is a quiet and effective compliment, and forgetting or misspelling it is a real handicap. Andrew Carnegie won a merger by proposing to call the new firm the Pullman Palace Car Company.

   People care so much about their names that they pay large sums to perpetuate them, which is why libraries and museums carry names like Astor and Morgan. Remembering names is a learnable skill; most people fail only because they won't take the time. Napoleon III asked to have an unclear name repeated and spelled, used it several times, linked it to the face, and for important people wrote it down, studied it and tore up the paper.

4. **Be a good listener.** Attentive, interested listening makes people think you a good conversationalist more than anything you say. A guest at a dinner party who barely spoke while a botanist talked for hours was later described by the botanist as a most interesting conversationalist.

   Patient, sympathetic listening calms even the angriest complainer, who often wants mainly to feel important; interrupting or contradicting makes things worse. A telephone company troubleshooter listened to a raging customer for hours over four visits, after which the customer paid every bill and withdrew all complaints. Often a person in trouble wants only to be heard, not advised. Lincoln once summoned an old Springfield neighbor, talked for hours about emancipation, and sent the neighbor home without asking for an opinion.

5. **Get people talking about themselves.** The person in front of you is a hundred times more interested in their own wants and problems than in yours; their own toothache outweighs a distant disaster. Those who talk only of themselves become bores. Talk to people about themselves and they will listen for hours.

6. **Talk about what the other person treasures most.** Find out what interests someone and lead with it. Henry Duvernoy failed for four years to sell bread to a hotel manager, then talked about the manager's passion, the Hotel Greeters, and was soon asked for samples and prices.

7. **Make the other person feel important, and do it sincerely.** This is the most important law of human conduct, the Golden Rule in another form. Breaking it brings endless trouble; keeping it brings friends. A bored postal clerk, complimented on a fine head of hair, was left beaming.

   Give appreciation without expecting anything back. The reward is the feeling of having done something for someone who cannot repay it; anyone who praises only to extract a favor will fail. Use the small courtesies, "I'm sorry to trouble you" and "thank you," which smooth everyday life and mark good breeding. Nearly everyone feels superior to you in some way, so recognize, sincerely and subtly, their importance in their own small world.

   Sincere admiration for someone's hobby, possessions or achievements opens them up, because even great people crave small recognitions. James Adamson was warned there would be five minutes with George Eastman. Adamson admired the woodwork of Eastman's office and asked about Eastman's early struggles; they talked for two hours, Adamson won the $90,000 seating order, and the two became lifelong friends. Appreciation of this kind can change lives and draw out extraordinary generosity.

So start now, on the person nearest you.

> one can make more friends in two months by becoming genuinely interested in other people than one can in two years by trying to get other people interested in him.


## How to bring people around to your view

**People defend their beliefs to protect their self-esteem, so anything that feels like an attack makes them dig in.** We form our beliefs carelessly and then defend them fiercely, because they have become "my" beliefs. Most of our reasoning is a search for excuses to go on believing what we already believe. Handled gently, though, people will often admit an error on their own, and even take pride in admitting it. Someone who is told they overpaid for draperies will defend the purchase; the same person, hearing the draperies admired, may freely admit they paid too much.

1. **Avoid arguments.** You cannot win one. If you lose, you lose; if you win, the other person feels inferior, resents you and holds their opinion more firmly. Frank Gammond, a Shakespeare scholar, once sided with a banquet guest who wrongly said a Shakespeare quotation came from the Bible, and explained afterwards: why prove a man wrong at a festive occasion?

   When someone attacks your product or position, agree with them, and they have nothing left to argue against. Truck salesman Patrick O'Haire stopped fighting prospects who praised a rival truck and simply agreed it was good, which left them speechless and opened the way to his own case. Replacing argument with appreciation lets a stubborn opponent feel important: tax consultant Frederick Parsons praised an arrogant inspector's practical expertise, and the inspector let a $9,000 item stand. Give up small points, even ones that are clearly yours. Napoleon's valet always let Josephine win at billiards.

2. **Show respect for the other person's opinions. Never tell them they are wrong.** Saying so, in words, a look or a gesture, strikes at their intelligence and self-respect, and after that no logic will move them. Even the ablest of us are right only part of the time. A young attorney told a Supreme Court justice flatly that admiralty law had no statute of limitations; the attorney was right, the courtroom went cold, and the argument failed.

   If you must prove something, do it so quietly that no one feels it being done. Announcing that you will prove a point amounts to claiming to be smarter, and it rouses opposition before you begin. A phrase like "I may be wrong; let's examine the facts" disarms the other side, turns the dispute into a shared search, and invites them to concede that they may be wrong too.

   Drop dogmatic language. Franklin gave up words like "certainly" and "undoubtedly" for "I conceive" and "it so appears to me at present," and began each disagreement by noting the circumstances in which the other person's view would hold. Franklin's ideas met less resistance afterwards, despite poor speaking skills. You can also correct someone with friendly questions and leave the decision with them: lumber salesman R. V. Crowley asked an inexperienced inspector cooperative questions without suggesting any error, until the inspector admitted the inexperience and accepted the whole carload.

3. **If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.** Say the critical things the other person is thinking before they can say them. That leaves them only one way to feel important, which is to be generous, and they usually minimize your fault. Commercial artist Ferdinand Warren criticized a mistake before a fault-finding art director could, and the director ended up defending Warren and offering another commission.

   Owning a mistake raises you above the herd. Anyone can defend their errors. After Pickett's charge failed, Robert E. Lee rode out alone to meet the beaten troops and said that all of it was Lee's own fault.

4. **Begin in a friendly way.** People cannot be forced to agree, but they can be led by gentleness and goodwill; a hostile opening makes the other side ball up their fists too. John D. Rockefeller Jr. visited striking Colorado miners in their homes, opened a speech by calling them friends, and the strikers went back to work.

   Start with what you agree on, and keep stressing that you want the same end and differ only on method. Antagonizing people at the outset may feel satisfying, but it defeats any practical aim. Frame requests around what the other person wants, not what your institution requires: a bank teller, James Eberson, asked a reluctant depositor whether the money should go to next of kin, and the depositor gave every detail and opened a trust account.

5. **Get the other person saying "yes" at the start.** Once someone says "No," pride demands that they stay consistent, and their whole body sets itself against agreement. Early yeses build momentum. A Westinghouse salesman, Allison, got a chief engineer to agree on the industry temperature standard and the room temperature, then asked whether 147-degree water would scald a hand; the engineer agreed and ordered about $35,000 of motors.

   Socrates worked this way. Instead of telling people they were wrong, Socrates asked questions they had to agree with, collecting admission after admission, until they arrived at a conclusion they would have rejected at the start.

6. **Let the other person do most of the talking.** They know more about their business and problems than you do, and they won't listen while their own ideas are still bursting to get out. So ask questions, listen, and don't interrupt even when you disagree. A fabric salesman with laryngitis let a car company president do the talking, the president argued for the samples, and the salesman won a $1,600,000 contract.

   Some people cannot be sold; they have to buy. Joseph Webb asked a hostile farm woman, Mrs. Druckenbrod, to sell some eggs, praised her Dominick hens and listened, until she asked whether electric lights would pay in her hen house. Let others outshine you, too. When they excel us they feel important; when we excel them they feel inferior and envious. Charles Cubellis asked a prospective employer about starting out with desk room and one stenographer, listened to the long answer, and was hired.

7. **Let the other person feel the idea is theirs.** People trust ideas they discover more than ideas handed to them. Eugene Wesson failed 150 times to sell sketches to a buyer, then brought unfinished ones and asked how to complete them, and the buyer took them.

   Self-expression is a dominant human need, so people embrace what they help create: a three-year-old who refused cereal ate two helpings after making it. Asking for advice makes people feel important and turns them into willing buyers; an X-ray manufacturer asked a hospital doctor to evaluate imperfect new machines, and the doctor canceled a dinner to look and then ordered them. Lao Tzu described the posture: rivers and seas receive the homage of the mountain streams because they keep below them.

8. **Try honestly to see things from the other person's point of view.** They may be wrong, but they have reasons, and finding those reasons gives you the key to how they act. Take nothing else from all this and it could still change your career. Instead of ordering boys to put out a fire in a park, one park visitor said they too had loved building fires as a child, explained the danger, and asked them to use the sand pit; the boys complied without resentment.

   Prepare this way before any interview. Dean Donham of Harvard Business School would rather walk the sidewalk outside an office for two hours than walk in without a clear idea of what to say and how the other person was likely to answer.

9. **Be sympathetic with the other person's ideas and desires.** Say sincerely that in their place you would feel exactly as they do, which is true, since with their body, temperament and experience you would be them. Had you inherited Al Capone's body, mind and environment, you would be what Capone was. The irritated, unreasoning person deserves pity more than blame.

   When a letter infuriates you, write the harsh reply, lock it in a drawer for two days, and send a polite one instead; Taft did this, and a bitter mother who had written to Taft ended up apologizing. Almost everyone craves sympathy, as a child shows by displaying an injury. The impresario Hurok met the temperamental singer Chaliapin's complaints by sympathetically offering to cancel the performances, and Chaliapin would end up singing.

10. **Appeal to nobler motives.** As J. P. Morgan observed, a person usually has two reasons for doing a thing, one that sounds good and a real one. Everyone, criminals included, likes to think of themselves as fine and unselfish, so address the good-sounding motive and treat people as honest. James Thomas let six customers who disputed their bills adjust the bills themselves; five gave the company the benefit of the doubt, and all six later bought new cars.

11. **Dramatize your ideas.** Stating a truth is not enough; it has to be vivid. James Boynton's first market report turned into an argument over methods; on the next visit Boynton dumped thirty-two competing cold-cream jars on the client's desk, each tagged with its findings, and a ten-minute meeting ran over an hour.

12. **Throw down a challenge when nothing else works.** Spirited people want to excel more than they want pay or fear pressure. Charles Schwab chalked the day shift's number of heats, 6, on the floor of a lagging mill; the night shift answered with 7, the day shift with 10, and the mill became the plant's top producer.

> If you are going to prove anything, don't let anybody know it. Do it so subtly, so adroitly that no one will feel that you are doing it.


## Correcting people without giving offense

When you have to change someone's behavior, the danger is the same as with any criticism: you wound their pride and they resist. **The way through is to protect their dignity at every step, so that correcting the fault feels to them like a gain and not a defeat.**

1. **Begin with praise and honest appreciation.** Unpleasant things are easier to hear after our good points have been acknowledged, as a barber lathers before shaving. McKinley called a supporter's unsuitable speech splendid before asking for a rewrite.
2. **Call attention to mistakes indirectly.** A direct rebuke wounds pride; an indirect signal lets the person see the error and fix it. Schwab found men smoking under a "No smoking" sign, handed them cigars and asked them to smoke outside, and they admired the restraint.
3. **Talk about your own mistakes first.** Hearing your faults is easier when the critic admits to being far from perfect. When the Kaiser was enraged, von Bülow calmed things by humbling himself and praising the Kaiser's knowledge of science.
4. **Ask questions instead of giving orders.** A suggestion lets people correct themselves, saves their pride and makes them want to cooperate. Owen D. Young, by a colleague's account, never gave a direct order in three years, saying instead "You might consider this" or "What do you think of this?"
5. **Let the other person save face.** Even when you must demote, fire or defeat someone, a few considerate words remove the sting, while riding over their pride in front of others leaves lasting hurt. General Electric moved Steinmetz out of running a department by giving the title of Consulting Engineer.
6. **Praise the slightest improvement, and every improvement.** Animal trainers reward each small step, and people respond the same way; encouragement can wake the powers people leave unused. Caruso's mother praised Caruso's singing after a teacher said there was no voice there at all.
7. **Give people a fine reputation to live up to.** Treat the trait you want as one they already have, and say so; they will work hard rather than disappoint you. Mrs. Gent told a maid reputed to be sloppy that surely the house would be kept as neatly as the maid dressed, and the house was kept shining.
8. **Use encouragement, and make the fault seem easy to correct.** Tell someone they have no gift and must start over, and you destroy their reason to try; tell them they have a natural flair and it's easy, and they will practice. A man quit a dance teacher who said everything had to be relearned, then improved under one who praised his natural rhythm.
9. **Make the other person happy about doing what you suggest.** Shape requests, refusals and assignments so the person gains dignity from them. J. A. Want turned a complaining mechanic into the "Manager of the Service Department." Neglecting this can be costly; Wilson's failure here contributed to the failure of the League of Nations.

Asking for a small favor or for advice works in the same way, and it can even turn an enemy into a friend. The request flatters the other person's knowledge, whereas doing a favor for an enemy may only arouse suspicion. Benjamin Franklin won over a hostile legislator by asking to borrow a rare book. Ken Dyke's letters opening with "I wonder if you would mind helping me out of a little difficulty?" drew 42½ per cent replies, against the usual 5 to 8.

> It is always easier to listen to unpleasant things after we have heard some praise of our good points.


## Bringing it home

Everything above applies with double force in a family, where we tend to forget it. **Most people work hard at business and leave their marriage to chance, though a contented home matters more to happiness than money or fame.** Turgenev said he would give up all his genius and books for a woman who cared whether he came home late for dinner.

1. **Don't nag.** Nagging is the deadliest destroyer of love, and it always makes things worse, even when the complaints are justified. The only question is whether it helped, and it never does. Countess Tolstoi's complaints drove Tolstoi to flee into the snow, and Tolstoi died eleven days later.
2. **Don't try to remake your partner.** Let them be happy in their own way, as long as it doesn't do violence to yours. A good marriage depends less on finding the right person than on being one. Disraeli married an older widow, Mary Anne, who mixed up the Greeks and the Romans and had bizarre taste; Disraeli never criticized her, defended her fiercely, and said that in thirty years she had never been boring.
3. **Don't criticize.** Futile criticism is a leading reason marriages end in divorce. Gladstone, rather than scold, signaled that breakfast was overdue by chanting a mysterious song. With children, before you criticize, remember they are only children; constant faultfinding comes from measuring them by an adult's yardstick. In "Father Forgets," a father who scolded his son all day kneels in shame by the boy's bed after the child ran in for a goodnight hug.
4. **Give honest appreciation.** Thank your partner for their cooking, their looks and what they contribute, and say it aloud. This is where appreciation is most needed and most neglected. Couples once admired each other and stop saying so. A farm woman served her men a pile of hay because in twenty years none of them had shown that they noticed her cooking. Never criticize a spouse's housekeeping or compare it with a parent's; even on a bad day, a mild word that the meal isn't up to the usual standard does more good.
5. **Pay little attentions.** Marriages are made or lost in small daily gestures, not great tragedies, and most divorces come from love lost in little ways. Flowers, phone calls, remembered dates and a wave goodbye show you are thinking of someone. George M. Cohan phoned his mother twice a day.
6. **Be courteous.** We treat those closest to us more rudely than strangers, insulting them over trivial faults we would forgive in a customer. Rudeness eats away love. Leave the day's troubles outside the door, as the Dutch leave their shoes on the doorstep.
7. **Read a good book on the sexual side of marriage.** Surveys and experts put sexual maladjustment among the chief causes of marital failure, and Dr. Popenoe lists it first. Many couples simply don't know enough. Overcome reticence, discuss it frankly, and educate yourselves from sound books; Rev. Butterfield had every couple he married do exactly that.

> It was not that I did not love you; it was that I expected too much of youth.
