Dale Carnegie's argument is that dealing with people is a skill, that it matters more to success and happiness than technical knowledge, and that anyone can learn it. The skill is a set of habits. Stop criticizing. Give honest appreciation. Take a real interest in other people. When you want someone to do something, make him want it. Under all of it lies one observation: every person is hungry to feel important, and almost nobody feeds that hunger. Feed it honestly and you win cooperation at work and peace at home. Feed it falsely and you are a flatterer, and you will be found out. The book is anecdotal and dated in places, and it repeats itself. Its opening chapters on criticism and appreciation, and its long middle on winning people to your way of thinking, are the parts worth reading whole.

## Where the book came from

The book began in a classroom. Carnegie taught public speaking to adults, and Lowell Thomas, who describes those courses, makes the case for them. A man who talks well is lifted above the crowd and is often credited with more ability than he has. Leadership drifts toward whoever can speak, so the training pays in standing and income. Godfrey Meyer, a banker, was struck dumb in his first class. Later he spoke at a town meeting about his tax bill and was elected to the town council at the head of ninety-six candidates.

Carnegie made every student talk at every session, before a sympathetic audience. Repetition wore the fear down, and the courage that replaced it carried into business and private life. He came to see his real job as helping people conquer fear, with speaking only the means. You build self-confidence by doing the thing you fear and getting a record of successes behind you. Patrick O'Haire paced outside a customer's office half a dozen times before he dared go in. The course cured his fear of audiences and of customers, and he became a star salesman. Carnegie also believed almost anyone could speak acceptably given self-confidence and an idea boiling inside him, because eloquence comes from conviction more than talent. Knock the most ignorant man in town down, he says, and he will get up talking with the eloquence of William Jennings Bryan.

The claim that gives the book its subject comes next. Even in technical fields, skill with people counts for far more than technical knowledge. Carnegie cites Carnegie Foundation research putting about 15 per cent of financial success down to technical knowledge and "85 per cent is due to skill in human engineering." Technical skill is cheap. Knowledge joined to the power to express ideas and rouse enthusiasm commands the top pay. Rockefeller told Matthew C. Brush he would pay more for the ability to deal with people than for any other ability under the sun, and in Carnegie's classes the best-paid engineers were often not the ones who knew most engineering.

Adults wanted guidance and could not find it. A University of Chicago and Y.M.C.A. survey of every adult in Meriden, Connecticut, found that after health their chief interest was people: how to understand them, be liked, and win them over. Schools taught Latin and mathematics and nothing on this, and no suitable textbook existed. So Carnegie built one from practice. He lectured on a principle, students tried it in their lives and reported back, and over fifteen years the rules grew from a postcard into a book. One employer of 314 people had always criticized them. He changed his ways, and his workers became friends who greeted him and called him by his first name.

Carnegie borrows William James's view that we are only half awake and use a small part of our resources, and he means the book to rouse the rest. It is a book of action. Following Spencer, he holds that the aim of education is action, and he sets a test: if the reader is no better equipped for life after the first three chapters, he will count the book a total failure.

To pass the test the reader needs four things. The first is a deep, driving desire to learn, kept alive by remembering how much popularity, happiness and income depend on handling people. The second is rereading, since we forget fast: read each chapter twice and review the book monthly, as an insurance office manager rereads every contract. Carnegie admits he must reread his own book on speaking. The third is practice, because this is habit and not information. "Only knowledge that is used sticks in your mind." Criticizing and talking of our own wants come naturally, so pause before acting on impulse and use the book as a handbook. The fourth is a weekly review. A Wall Street bank president with little schooling went through his engagement book every Saturday evening, asking what mistakes he had made and what he could learn, and credited most of his success to it.


## The hunger nobody feeds

The argument proper starts with criminals. People almost never blame themselves, however wrong they are. Two Gun Crowley, who shot a policeman, wrote that he had a kind heart that would do nobody harm. Al Capone saw himself as a misunderstood public benefactor. Few men in Sing Sing thought themselves bad. If killers rationalize, the people we live and work with will too, and any approach that needs them to condemn themselves will fail.

So criticism is futile. It produces self-justification, and the blame comes back to the critic like a homing pigeon. When Theodore Roosevelt denounced Taft, Taft said tearfully that he did not see how he could have done differently. Albert Fall and his wife insisted he was the one betrayed. Criticism is also dangerous, because it wounds pride and the resentment can last a lifetime. A scribbled rebuke from Richard Harding Davis left Carnegie resenting him for years after Davis died. Criticism made Thomas Hardy quit fiction and drove Chatterton to suicide. The reason is that we are not dealing with creatures of logic but with creatures of emotion, prejudice, pride and vanity.

Lincoln learned this slowly. A ridiculing letter once nearly led him into a sword duel with James Shields. Years later, after Meade let Lee escape, Lincoln wrote Meade a severe letter and never sent it; it turned up among his papers after his death. He reasoned that with Meade's timid temperament he might have done the same. Carnegie adds that anger should cool first. The German army made a soldier with a complaint sleep on it before filing. Better still, improve yourself before others; it is more profitable and less dangerous. Confucius said not to complain of the snow on your neighbor's roof while your own doorstep is unclean. Above all, try to understand people. "Any fool can criticize, condemn, and complain," but understanding takes character and breeds tolerance. Benjamin Franklin, tactless in youth, resolved to speak ill of no man and became adroit enough to be made ambassador to France.

The positive principle follows. The only way to get anyone to do anything is to make him want to do it. A revolver gets you a man's watch and a threat of firing gets cooperation until your back is turned, but force is brief and costly. "Every act you ever performed since the day you were born is because you wanted something." A hundred dollars given to the Red Cross buys the giver a feeling he wanted more than the money.

What people want most, after food and sleep, is to feel important. Dewey called it the deepest urge in human nature, and Carnegie adds Freud, Lincoln and James. His father displayed his prize hogs' blue ribbons on a muslin sheet; the hogs did not care. Washington, Columbus and Catherine the Great all wanted grand titles. Some people fall ill for this feeling. Mrs. McKinley made the President neglect affairs of state to soothe her, and one woman stayed in bed ten years until her mother died. Carnegie suggests some people go insane to find importance reality denied them, like the asylum patient who calls herself Lady Smith. We would never starve our families of food for days, yet we starve them of appreciation for years. Carnegie fasted six days without great trouble; Alfred Lunt said he needed nothing so much as nourishment for his self-esteem.

The same urge built civilization. "If our ancestors hadn’t had this flaming urge for a feeling of importance, civilization would have been impossible." It sent Lincoln, a poor grocery clerk, to law books found in a barrel, and lay behind Dickens and Wren. How a man satisfies it is his character. Rockefeller funded a hospital in Peking; Dillinger robbed banks and announced who he was; young criminals ask for the newspapers that make them heroes.

Hence the big secret: "imagine what miracles you and I can achieve by giving people honest appreciation this side of insanity." Whoever honestly meets this hunger holds people in the palm of his hand. Charles Schwab, paid a million dollars a year chiefly for his skill with people, praised lavishly and never criticized: "and the way to develop the best that is in a man is by appreciation and encouragement." Most men do the reverse. Andrew Carnegie praised his associates everywhere, even in the epitaph he wrote himself. When Edward Bedford lost a million dollars in South America, Rockefeller congratulated him on saving sixty per cent of the investment.

This is not flattery. "One is sincere and the other insincere." Flattery is cheap praise, telling a man what he already thinks of himself. General Obregon's bust warns against friends who flatter, and King George V kept a maxim refusing cheap praise. It can work on the starved: the Mdivani brothers won famous wives with it, and Disraeli laid it on with a trowel for Queen Victoria. But Disraeli was a genius, and for the rest of us flattery is counterfeit that ends in trouble. The cure is to stop thinking about ourselves, which fills about 95 per cent of our idle thought, and look for the other man's good points, as Emerson did, finding every man his superior in some way. Carnegie insists the principles work only from the heart. He offers a new way of life, not a bag of tricks.

The last foundation is wants. Nobody cares what you want, so talk about what he wants and show him how to get it. Carnegie loves strawberries and cream but baits his hook with worms. The Emersons could not push a calf into the barn; the housemaid led it in sucking her finger. A father got his son to eat by promising he could beat the neighborhood bully. Business letters break this rule. An advertising agency boasted of its size and demanded a reply; a freight terminal described its own congestion and reached the customer's benefit only at the end, so Carnegie rewrites it around the customer. A real-estate man would not make a one-minute call for Carnegie but asked for his insurance; a throat specialist asked his income before examining him. People want problems solved and like to feel they are buying.

So, as Overstreet advises, first arouse an eager want. Andrew Carnegie got silent nephews to reply by return mail with a postscript saying he was sending money, which he did not enclose. Demands start fights. Faced with a 300 per cent rent increase, Carnegie pictured storming in and saw the argument the manager's pride could never concede. Instead he listed the manager's own advantages and losses, and the increase fell to 50 per cent. Henry Ford said success "lies in the ability to get the other person’s point of view and see things from his angle as well as from your own." Few do it, so the rare person who unselfishly serves others has little competition, and Owen D. Young said such a man need never worry about the future.


## How to be liked

Carnegie's six rules for being liked rest on one claim: you make more friends in two months by being genuinely interested in others than in two years by trying to interest them in you. His dog Tippy won affection by pure interest. Howard Thurston told himself before every show that he loved his audience. Theodore Roosevelt greeted every White House servant by name. People are not interested in you. "They are interested in themselves." A telephone company study found *I* the commonest word in calls, and everyone looks for his own face first in a group photograph. Alfred Adler held that the man uninterested in others has the greatest difficulties and does the greatest harm, and a Collier's editor said readers would not like stories by an author who did not like people.

Interest opens doors. Famous authors and cabinet members spoke at Carnegie's classes after students wrote of admiring them, and a banker got vital information by bringing stamps for an executive's son. Interest costs effort: the Prince of Wales studied Spanish for months before touring South America, and Carnegie sends friends a letter or telegram on their birthdays. It shows in the greeting too; telephone operators were trained to ask for the number in a tone glad to serve. C. M. Knaphle tried for years to sell coal to a chain-store executive he resented. Needing facts for a debate on chain stores, he asked the man for help. The executive talked for nearly two hours and then offered to buy coal unprompted. Knaphle said two hours of interest in the man did more than ten years of talk about coal. Publilius Syrus knew it long before.

The second rule is to smile, sincerely, since a mechanical grin is resented. A smile says I like you and I am glad to see you. The stockbroker William B. Steinhardt smiled at his wife, doorman and colleagues for a week and found his marriage, his complaints and his earnings transformed. A rubber-company chairman saw men succeed while having a rip-roaring good time and fail once work grew dull; you must enjoy meeting people if you want them to enjoy meeting you. If you do not feel like smiling, act as if you do. James held that action is under more direct control of the will, so regulating action regulates feeling. Happiness is an inner attitude. "It is what you think about it." Carnegie watched thirty or forty disabled boys climbing station stairs on Long Island, laughing, and was told such boys usually grow happier than others once past the shock.

The third rule is names. A man cares more for his own name than for all others combined. Jim Farley could call fifty thousand people by first name and helped elect Roosevelt. Andrew Carnegie named a steel works after the Pennsylvania Railroad's president and won a merger by proposing to call it the Pullman Palace Car Company. Barnum offered his grandson twenty-five thousand dollars to call himself Barnum Seeley, and libraries carry the names of Astor and Morgan. Most people forget names only because they won't concentrate and repeat. Roosevelt remembered a mechanic's name heard once. Napoleon III had unclear names spelled, used them, tied them to faces, and wrote important ones down, studied them and tore up the paper.

The fourth rule is to listen. Carnegie barely spoke while a botanist talked for hours, and was called a most interesting conversationalist. To be interesting, be interested. Listening calms anger. A telephone trouble-shooter heard out a raging customer over four visits, and the man paid his bills and dropped his complaints. Julian Detmer let an angry debtor talk himself out, thanked him, and kept him for life. Such people often want only importance, and some want only an ear. Lincoln talked for hours to an old Springfield neighbor about emancipation, never asked his opinion, and seemed easier afterward.

The fifth rule is to talk in terms of the other man, who cares more for his own toothache than for distant disasters. Carnegie's recipe for being shunned is to talk about yourself and interrupt. A bigamist who won twenty-three women told Dorothy Dix, from jail, that you need only talk to a woman about herself; Disraeli said "talk to a man about himself and he will listen for hours." The royal road to a man's heart is what he treasures most. Henry Duvernoy failed for four years to sell bread to a hotel manager, then talked about the Hotel Greeters, the man's passion, and was soon asked for prices. Roosevelt read up the night before on each visitor's interests.

The sixth rule is the supreme law: always make the other person feel important. Carnegie ties it to Dewey, James and the Golden Rule. He praised a bored postal clerk's hair and a Radio City clerk's diction and left both beaming, wanting nothing back but the feeling of having given something. Those who praise only for gain have souls no bigger than sour crab apples. Courtesy belongs here; tell a waitress who brings the wrong potatoes that you are sorry to trouble her and she will gladly fix it. Nearly everyone feels superior in some way, as Japanese, Hindus and Eskimos each look down on Westerners, so recognize a man's importance in his small world, sincerely. Hall Caine's admiring lecture on Rossetti led to a secretaryship and a fortune; an attorney who admired an old widow's house was offered her husband's Packard. James Adamson, given five minutes with George Eastman, admired his office woodwork and asked about his early struggles. They talked two hours, lunched, and Adamson won the $90,000 order and a lifelong friend. Even a man worth a hundred million craved small recognitions. McMahon praised a judge's dogs and got a valuable puppy. Carnegie tells the reader to shut the book and try appreciation on the nearest person.


## Winning people to your way of thinking

This is the richest part of the book, and Carnegie's twelve rules for persuasion begin with a refusal.

1. Avoid arguments. You cannot win one; the loser resents you and holds his view harder. At a banquet Carnegie corrected a man who credited Shakespeare to the Bible, and his friend Frank Gammond, a Shakespeare scholar, sided with the man, asking afterward why prove anyone wrong at a festive occasion. Patrick O'Haire stopped fighting prospects who praised a rival truck and simply agreed, leaving them speechless. Frederick Parsons praised an arrogant tax inspector's expertise and saw a $9,000 item allowed. Napoleon's valet Constant let Josephine win at billiards, and Lincoln told an officer: "Better give your path to a dog than be bitten by him in contesting for the right. Even killing the dog would not cure the bite."

2. Respect the other man's opinions; never say he is wrong. "That will make him want to strike back. But it will never make him want to change his mind." A young attorney told a Supreme Court justice there was no statute of limitations in admiralty law, and though right, he lost the room. Prove things so quietly that nobody notices, as Chesterfield advised his son. We defend beliefs, Carnegie says after James Harvey Robinson, to protect self-esteem: "The little word “my” is the most important one in human affairs." Criticized for overpaying for draperies, Carnegie defended them; admired for them, he confessed. The magic phrase is I may be wrong. "Let’s examine the facts." Stefansson said a scientist only seeks facts. Franklin, told by an old Quaker friend that his opinions slapped everyone, dropped *certainly* for *it so appears to me at present* and became America's great diplomat. R. V. Crowley asked an inexperienced lumber inspector friendly questions until the man admitted his error and took the carload, and F. J. Mahoney let an angry customer choose the design at his own risk and won more orders.

3. If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically. Caught with his dog unleashed in Forest Park, Carnegie confessed first and the policeman excused him. Ferdinand Warren condemned his own mistake before a fault-finding art director could, and was defended and rehired. Lee rode out after Pickett's charge and took the blame; Elbert Hubbard told angry readers he did not quite agree with his article either.

4. Begin in a friendly way. A drop of honey, Carnegie quotes, is the high road to a man's reason. Rockefeller Jr. called striking Colorado miners friends, and they returned to work. O. L. Straub praised his landlord and got his rent cut; Mrs. Day praised her maître d'hôtel and got superb service. "The sun can make you take off your coat more quickly than the wind." A doctor wrote the Boston Herald's publisher praising his family paper and asking how to explain its abortionist advertisements to a daughter, and the ads went. Stress shared ends. Overstreet calls antagonizing people at the start to feel important psychologically stupid. James Eberson asked a balky depositor whether he wanted his money to reach his next of kin, and the man filled in everything and opened a trust for his mother.

5. Get the other person saying yes at once, because pride keeps a man loyal to his no. Allison of Westinghouse won agreement on temperature standards and asked whether 147-degree water would scald a hand, and sold about $35,000 of motors. Socrates gathered an armful of yeses until opponents reached conclusions they would have denied.

6. Let the other man do the talking. "Let the other man talk himself out. He knows more about his business and his problems than you do." A salesman with laryngitis let a car company president argue for his samples and won a $1,600,000 contract. Joseph Webb admired Mrs. Druckenbrod's Dominick hens until she asked whether electric lights would pay in her hen house. Let others excel you: Charles Cubellis got a job by asking an employer about his start with one stenographer, and Irvin Cobb said he had been more fortunate than he deserved.

7. Let him feel the idea is his. Eugene Wesson failed 150 times until he asked a buyer how to finish his sketches. Colonel House let Wilson present House's ideas. A child ate cereal she cooked herself; an X-ray maker asked a doctor's advice and got an order; a car dealer let a customer appraise a car and buy it at his own figure. "So the sage, wishing to be above men, putteth himself below them; wishing to be before them, he putteth himself behind them."

8. Try honestly to see his point of view. "There is a reason why the other man thinks and acts as he does." Carnegie stopped ordering boys to douse park fires, recalled his own boyhood fires, and asked them to use the sand pit. Dean Donham would rather walk outside an office for two hours than enter unprepared for the other man's answers.

9. Be sympathetic. With Capone's body and surroundings you would be Capone; John B. Gough said so of himself. An angry woman who scolded Carnegie over Louisa May Alcott's home ended by apologizing. Taft locked away his harsh reply to a bitter mother and sent a polite one. Arthur Gates says the species craves sympathy, and Hurok won over Chaliapin by offering to cancel his concerts.

10. Appeal to nobler motives, since "a man usually has two reasons for doing a thing: one that sounds good and a real one." Even Jesse James likely saw himself as an idealist. James Thomas let six disputing customers adjust their own bills; five favored the company and all six bought new cars. Northcliffe told the press his mother did not like a photograph.

11. Dramatize your ideas. The Philadelphia Evening Bulletin printed a day's reading as a 307-page book, *One Day*. James Boynton dumped thirty-two tagged cold-cream jars on a client's desk and turned ten minutes into an hour.

12. Throw down a challenge. Schwab chalked the day shift's six heats on the mill floor; the night shift made seven, the day shift ten. Al Smith told Lewis Lawes it took a big man to stay at Sing Sing, and Lawes went.


## Correcting people without offense

The leadership rules apply the same thinking to correction. Begin with praise, as a barber lathers before he shaves. McKinley called a supporter's unsuitable speech splendid before asking for a rewrite, Lincoln praised Hooker's bravery before naming his faults, and W. P. Gaw admired a bronze maker's plant and got his delivery promised unasked. Point out mistakes indirectly. Schwab handed cigars to men smoking under a No Smoking sign and asked them to smoke outside, and they admired his silence. Lyman Abbott's wife said his stiff sermon would make a fine magazine article, and he preached without notes.

Talk about your own mistakes first. Carnegie recalled his blunders before correcting his niece Josephine, and von Bülow soothed the raging Kaiser by humbling himself and praising the Kaiser's science. Ask questions instead of giving orders; Owen D. Young, a colleague said, never gave a direct order in three years. The method saves pride. "It makes him want to co-operate instead of rebel." Let people save face. General Electric made Steinmetz Consulting Engineer when moving him out of a department, Marshall Granger praised seasonal staff as he let them go, and Kemal told surrendering Greek generals they must be tired.

Praise every improvement, as trainers reward animals. Caruso's mother praised his singing against a teacher's verdict; an editor's praise transformed Dickens; a schoolmaster rescued H. G. Wells from a drapery shop; Rupert Hughes sent Lawrence Tibbett to New York. Give a man a reputation to live up to. Mrs. Gent told a sloppy maid she surely kept house as neatly as she dressed, and Georgette Leblanc told Marie the Dishwasher she had treasures within her. Warden Lawes did the same with crooks. Make faults seem easy to fix. A man quit a dance teacher who said he must relearn everything and thrived under one who praised his rhythm, and Ely Culbertson's wife convinced him he had a gift for bridge. Make people happy about what you ask. Colonel House told Bryan he was too prominent to be peace emissary, J. A. Want made a grumbling mechanic Manager of the Service Department, and Wilson's League failed partly for neglect of this. Finally, ask small favors. Ken Dyke's letter asking for help with a little difficulty drew 42½ per cent replies against the usual 5 to 8. Franklin borrowed a rare book from a hostile legislator and gained a friend. A plumber who snarled at salesmen, asked by Mr. Amsel for advice on a branch store, talked an hour, ordered heavily, and became his golfing friend.


## Home

The book closes with seven rules for family life. Do not nag. Carnegie calls it the deadliest device for destroying love. "It never fails." Eugénie's nagging sent Napoleon III out a side door at night, Countess Tolstoi's drove Tolstoi into the snow to die eleven days later, and Mary Todd Lincoln's rages kept Lincoln on the circuit. Real grievances do not matter; nagging never helped.

Do not remake your partner. Disraeli married an older widow who confused Greeks and Romans, never criticized her, defended her, and said she never bored him in thirty years. Do not criticize. Gladstone chanted a mysterious song rather than complain about breakfast, and Catherine the Great ate burned meat in silence. With children, Carnegie offers *Father Forgets*, where a father kneels by his son's bed ashamed that he "expected too much of youth."

Give honest appreciation, most needed and most neglected at home. A farm woman served her men hay after twenty years without thanks. Warner Baxter gave his wife, who left the stage, his applause. Dorothy Dix told bridegrooms never to criticize a wife's housekeeping. "Let her know that you appreciate the fact." Marriage matters more to happiness than business, yet few men work at it; Turgenev would have traded his genius for a woman who cared when he came home. Pay little attentions. George M. Cohan phoned his mother twice a day, Judge Sabbath thought a wife's morning wave would prevent many divorces, and Browning's small tributes made Elizabeth feel an angel. Be courteous, since "it’s only the members of our own family, those who are nearest and dearest to us, that we dare insult for their trivial faults." Holmes hid his blues from his family; the Dutch leave shoes at the door. Last, Dr. Popenoe ranks sexual maladjustment first among causes of failed marriages, and Rev. Butterfield gave couples books. Carnegie's advice is to drop the reticence and read sound ones.
