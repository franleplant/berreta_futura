# What this magazine is for

Transmit the information in a source to a human reader in the most efficient and
effective way we can, with style: capture the key ideas, and trim the fat.

Everything else in `prompts/` and `docs/` serves that sentence. When a rule and
this page disagree, this page wins and the rule is wrong.

## What that means in practice

**The reader would not have read the source.** They are a working developer or
manager with far more coming at them than they can read. The realistic
alternative to our version is not the original — it is nothing. Condensing
faithfully is the product, not a shortfall in it.

**Key ideas, not coverage.** A piece is not a smaller copy of its source. Decide
what the source is actually saying, and give the reader that, whole. Everything
that does not serve the key ideas is fat, however true it is: background the
argument never uses, a second example that teaches what the first taught,
the setup steps, the version history, the throat-clearing.

**Efficient.** Every paragraph earns its space or goes. A reader should never
work to find the point, and never reread a sentence to parse it.

**Effective.** They should finish holding something: a mechanism they can now
picture, a distinction they will notice next week, a claim that changes what
they do. Accurate prose that leaves them with nothing has failed here.

**With style.** `docs/WRITING_STYLE.md` is not decoration on top of the
information — it is how the information lands. Feynman builds the model, Orwell
strips the language, Hemingway picks the detail and holds the temperature.

## The failures this is written against

Real pieces, from real runs:

- An article that carried its source's argument for nine paragraphs and then
  became a setup manual: `npx` install lines, CLI phase names, a truncated
  repository URL. Accurate, unusable, and the reason the piece felt pointless.
- An explainer on a protocol that left a reader no better able to explain the
  protocol: every term defined, nothing understood.
- Seven pages of unbroken prose with no section headings, because nothing
  required them.
- A judge that spent three rounds demanding install commands be restored to a
  printed page.

None of those are failures of accuracy. All of them are failures of
transmission, which is the only thing we are actually being paid in attention
for.

## What is not the standard

Not length or ratio: a tenth of the source can be right and two thirds can be
right. Not novelty: an original observation the reader cannot use is worth
nothing. Not coverage: naming everything the source mentions is how a piece
becomes a list. Not fidelity to the source's *arrangement*: if the source's
order is the clearest path, take it and be glad; if it is not, build a better
one. Fidelity is owed to what is true, never to a running order.
