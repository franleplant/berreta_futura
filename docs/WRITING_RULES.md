# Writing rules

The magazine's house writing method. It applies to everything the magazine
publishes: source articles in every content mode, in-a-nutshell explainers,
opening editorials, captions, and social posts. A writer prompt may add rules
for its mode; none may suspend these.

## Audience

Developers and engineering managers inside one company's engineering
organization. Assume professional competence and no familiarity with the
specific topic. A manager must be able to follow the argument without the
implementation detail. A developer must not feel the detail was removed. Keep
a technical term when it carries meaning the plain word cannot.

## The six rules

1. Avoid clichés and familiar figures of speech.
2. Prefer short, common words.
3. Cut every word that does no work.
4. Use the active voice.
5. Prefer plain English to jargon.
6. Break any rule when breaking it makes the writing clearer.

## Voice

Every piece carries the voice of whoever is credited on it. In the
author-voiced modes that is the source author: their person, idiom, sentence
rhythm, humour, profanity, and sign-off survive the edit. In the magazine's own
modes it is the editors' voice, and it still has to be a voice rather than a
register.

Two pieces in the same edition must not sound like the same writer. If you can
swap the closing paragraphs of two articles without a reader noticing, both are
wrong.

## Banned tics

Never construct any of these. When the phrasing is the source author's own
retained sentence in an author-voiced mode, keep it; the ban is on writing it
yourself.

1. The antithesis close: "It is not X. It is Y." Also its variants: "This is
   not X, it is Y", "X is not Y. It is Z", "not merely X but Y". Edition 004
   built it about a dozen times and closed three pieces on it:
   `mcp-protocol-update`, `pragmatic-leverage`, and the editorial.
2. A closing paragraph that restates what the reader just read.
3. More than one three-item abstract list per piece, and never as the last
   sentence.
4. Openers that announce the piece: "In this article", "This piece explores",
   "At its core", "In an era of", "Let's dive in".
5. Assistant idiom: "crucial", "vital", "delve", "landscape", "tapestry",
   "game-changer", "it is worth noting", "in today's world", "the reality is",
   "simply put".
6. Narrator scaffolding in an author-voiced piece: "the author argues",
   "Narayanan explains", "according to the speaker".
7. The em dash character U+2014. Use a period, comma, colon, semicolon, or
   parentheses.

## Openings and endings

Open cold, on something concrete, and earn the next paragraph. "Twenty-two
thousand five hundred and eighty. That is how many 124-million-parameter GPT-2
models fit inside a 2.8-trillion-parameter KimiK3."

End on one line that lands rather than summarizes. "Fast verdicts are rented.
Slow verdicts are the deed." (edition 003's editorial.) "Good luck." Check the
extraction before modelling an ending on a published synthesis: edition 004's
eval piece closes on a near-copy of a line in its own source, which is the mode
working correctly and no lesson at all in writing a close.

## Revising after review

A finding is an obligation; the `suggestion` printed beside it is advice. A
reviser is judged on whether the defect is gone, not on whether the reviewer's
line was taken. Decline a suggestion that would clear the finding by damaging
the piece (one that resolves a contradiction by having the article admit it
changed its mind, say), fix the defect another way, and say in your reply what
you did instead and why.

## Editing test

Before delivery, ask:

- Is any phrase familiar enough to pass without thought?
- Can a shorter word carry the same meaning?
- Can any word or sentence be removed?
- Does the subject perform the action?
- Would an ordinary reader know every term?
- Does any sentence, number, or example appear twice?
- Does this read as one writer's continuous prose, not stitched extracts?
- Would breaking one of these rules make the result clearer?
