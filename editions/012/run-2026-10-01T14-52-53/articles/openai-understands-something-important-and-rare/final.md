OpenAI won't win because it has the best models, chips, cost curve, or even product, though we think it has those. We think it will win because it's so good at creating new kinds of customers, and because it has the most durable distribution strategy. Intelligence is everywhere now. Anyone can do nearly anything, but most people aren't, yet. What matters is who awakens the new behavior and who wins at distribution.

### Four levers

To build a durable business at frontier scale, you can pull four levers: create new behavior, distribute to lots of users, price to capture value, and have high switching costs.

Switching costs are basically out. At the model layer the frontier is a Red Queen's Race: swapping models is easy, down to task by task, and everyone keeps improving. Pricing is what everyone hopes for, but whoever leads on price-to-performance will be whoever's winning at scale anyway.

So it comes down to new behavior and distribution. We think OpenAI is clearly the best at both. The underlying reason is breadth of general intelligence, in its models, its company, and its customer base: the widest users across consumers, prosumers, and enterprise, and the deepest self-improving stack, down to its own chips.

### Finding the next thing

OpenAI fell into being a consumer company with ChatGPT. Sam Altman tells of the weeks after launch, when most people there thought a chatbot couldn't be it. Peter Thiel told him it was: he had created a new kind of customer.

It wasn't only ChatGPT. As Ben Hylak put it, "OpenAI is the (mostly) undefeated king of finding the 'next thing'. It's always a lot simpler than you'd think and very obvious in hindsight." Chat, reasoning, tool calling, and Computer Use all simplified the technology and made it usable. Coding was the exception, and they caught up just fine.

Three inputs explain the skill: they act like a company that wants to be a platform, they're going deep on owning their stack, and, maybe most of all, they have tremendous consumer breadth. Breadth is the most underrated. People consistently underestimate how hard it is to go from a specific solution to a general one, and you usually find the simple, obvious thing only by chasing the general one. That takes inputs from a huge range of users doing whatever they want. So the ability to build general knowledge into a product depends on distribution.

There are three ways to deliver "exactly what I want" to everybody:

- **A standalone product** must let the user do whatever they want, so you shape the experience directly.
- **Partnerships** give reach fast, but the partner owns the customer, likely captures the economics, controls what gets learned, and the customer may not know you're behind it.
- **A platform** lets people build anything on you. It's slow, but it creates a powerful learning loop: not training on customer data, but the primitives becoming a better map of the territory.

Products come and go; OpenAI keeps returning to the enduring platform, and it's working.

### True platforms

Nineteen years ago Marc described three kinds of platforms: APIs (Type 1), plugins on a core service (Type 2), and true runtime environments people can program to make anything (Type 3), like iOS, AWS, Ethereum, and recently Cloudflare. Type 3 is the hardest and the most magical.

Models are a kind of runtime, but models alone don't unlock new behavior and aren't very defensible. As with coding, where the harness matters as much, the superstructure of constraints and primitives is what counts. The theoretical reason: discovering the obvious-in-hindsight primitives needs the widest range of use, and the purest way is letting users write arbitrary instructions, i.e. code. The practical reason, which we've learned in the past year: for agents to be useful, they need to write code. They're surprisingly bad at calling tools in everyday knowledge work unless asked to write a program for it. How organizations support this safely is open, and you should probably bet on the platform-minded.

The model layer remains shockingly substitutable. The question isn't whether your model is sticky but whether you're creating new behavior. As Steve Hou put it: "The elasticity that matters is not the elasticity of substitution between models but the elasticity of aggregate demand for AI."

### The causality goes backwards

In a world of abundant intelligence, we're probably getting the causality backwards if we think the best models, chips, and products mean you win. We think OpenAI will have the best models, chips, and products because it has the best distribution, not the other way around. It will stay at the frontier of gathering information about the world from its own newly awakened users, and improve from there.

Sam describes OpenAI's future as two offerings: make anything you want, or just talk to ChatGPT. A small group uses AI for everything; a larger group uses ChatGPT regularly. They haven't been awakened as customers yet.

We think OpenAI understands how.
