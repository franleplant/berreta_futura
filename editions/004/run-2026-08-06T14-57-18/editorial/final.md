**Ask What Exists**

A stranger comes to the door with what you need. You share no history and no language. This is not an edge case. It is most of working life.

The common answer is to assume. We assume the other side recalls Tuesday's decision, knows which system is authoritative, understands what "the report" means. Assumption is cheap in the moment and expensive every hour after.

There is another way, older than any machine. Say what you can do. Ask what is there. Then ask for the one thing you want, by its exact name.

Three sentences. Most meetings would end in four minutes.

The failures of ordinary speech are failures of the same kind. The padded clause, the passive that hides the actor, the sentence gesturing at a plan without naming one — each asks the listener to supply what the speaker withheld. It looks like courtesy. It is a transfer of labor, and usually of blame. The man who says mistakes were made has not communicated. He has installed a fog and walked out through it.

Clarity is not decoration. It is the willingness to be held to what you said.

The harder discipline is to expect nothing to be remembered. This offends us. We want our context carried forward, our earlier explanation to still count. But the colleague sat in four other conversations, the reader arrived at paragraph three, the line dropped. State the version of the world you are working from, every time. It costs a sentence. It saves the week.

Picture a library whose catalog is rewritten each night. The scholar who memorized it last spring walks the aisles with perfect confidence and is perfectly lost. The one who asks at the desk each morning goes straight to the shelf. The second scholar knows less and finds more.

So: declare, ask, name. Say the version. Say the thing. Do not make the other person guess, and do not punish them for asking.

It is not elegant. It works, and it keeps working after you have left the room.
