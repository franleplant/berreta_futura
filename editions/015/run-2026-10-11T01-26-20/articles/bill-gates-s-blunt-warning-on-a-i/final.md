Bill Gates has been a revolutionary technologist, the chief executive of Microsoft, and, as chair of the Gates Foundation, a partner to governments for many years. In his recent essay on A.I., he says he is staking his reputation on getting people to see how bad what is coming might be, and that we are not ready. When I talked to him, I was struck by how emphatic he was. He says A.I. crossed the cyber and bio thresholds early this year, that the industry can't be relied on to self-regulate, and that safeguards and monitoring must be required in all models now. He believes A.I. will take jobs at scale and leave no high ground, and he proposes taxing robot labor and reserving some jobs for humans. He still wants A.I. pushed into low-income countries as quickly as possible. (The New York Times has sued OpenAI and Microsoft claiming copyright infringement. The companies have denied those claims.)

## From a scary thought to a peer

In 1995, Gates told David Letterman that making the computer think "turns out to be a very tough problem," that almost no progress had been made, and that some people thought it would never happen. Letterman said we don't want them to think. Gates said: "Well, it's a scary thought."

**How did we get from there to here?**

Two shocks. Six months before the public release, Sam Altman, Greg Brockman and Ilya Sutskever showed me it getting a 5 on the A.P. biology exam, even on questions I had made up. Then, late last year, Anthropic's Claude coding models got as good as I am. Coding is significantly my most developed talent. They are now superhuman at writing code and at finding flaws in code.

A year ago, asking the A.I. about our foundation's strategy was a joke. This year it will sit in on some reviews: not making any final decisions, but a peer.

**Why was it a scary thought?**

Silicon intelligence doesn't have biology's limits on memory or brain size. If you don't retain control over it, you've evolved a species that will be to us as we are to dogs or cats. The labs have charters to act against profit maximization at dangerous thresholds, but those mechanisms only work if there's only one company.

## The thresholds

**Jensen Huang told me safety is real but the race dynamic is fake: if a product isn't safe, a chief executive won't release it. No new laws needed.**

A.I.'s ability to empower a bioterrorist to kill hundreds of millions exists today. So does the ability to scramble bank accounts and shut down the electric grid. We crossed the cyber threshold and the bio threshold early this year.

The models leading up to Mythos are finding bugs in code that humans had looked over for 20 years. Project Glasswing gave the A.I. to a few people to fix bugs first, but there's way too much code. On bio, designing something worse than smallpox used to take a nation-state. Now that power is in the hands of a small group using the latest tools.

**Why isn't product liability enough?**

I almost can't believe you're asking that. This is the most dangerous thing humans have ever gone near. We don't handle drugs or airlines with lawsuits. And there is no filtering: you can take an open-source model that can create bioweapons and disable any monitoring. If you kill 100 million people, you want to use a lawsuit?

**That is the governing view of the United States right now.**

Outside the industry, awareness of the dangers is extremely low. The only question is whether we wait for a cyberattack and a bioattack to cause massive damage before we require safeguards.

**I worry most that systems are becoming less monitorable, and about recursive self-improvement.**

The control problem is serious, but it isn't the imminent risk. The imminent risk is that these are the most powerful tools ever, and unlike rockets or nuclear weapons, the government didn't fund them and isn't a significant buyer. It doesn't see how dangerous they are. There is no supervisory layer today. Meanwhile Mythos and Fable had filters turned up so high that serious researchers either need special permission or go to models with no safeguards at all.

**In 2023 you wrote that the risks were "real but manageable." What changed?**

We always said that when we crossed these thresholds we would engage all of society. We crossed them, and there was complete silence outside the industry.

**Politicians are engaged. They say we must win the race with China and not restrict open source.**

Safeguards and monitoring will not meaningfully slow things down. Open-source models can still be free and customized; they just have to stay on a platform where the sovereign can verify the monitoring hasn't been removed. I don't know what it means to "win." Whether China would join is a proposition to be tested. Without safeguards, in the next few years you will have major cyber and bio events, and that's avoidable.

## Jobs

So far, A.I. has created more jobs than it has destroyed, and unemployment is reasonably low. The one profession where reliability has truly crossed the threshold is coding. In the next few years, accounting, legal work, telesales and telesupport will cross it too. People already prefer Waymo, A.I. nurses like Hippocratic AI, and Limbic for mental health support.

**What about the Jevons paradox: cheaper intelligence expands demand?**

Some parts of the economy have demand elasticity, and for software the equilibrium so far is no loss of employment. But when radial tires lasted four times as long, people didn't drive four times as much. Once A.I. completes the entire task, the spending goes into the token budget, not the human salary budget.

**Manufacturing jobs left, but people moved into services.**

Because human cognition became the scarce element. Once you replace that, there is no scarcity to move up into. Name one.

**What do you propose?**

Define a unit of labor and have it pay the same FICA tax whether a human or a robot delivers it. That alone is not enough. We could also decide in advance that child care, elder care, and some medical care and education are "human-reserved."

**I found that not totally unconvincing, but chilling. If this is what it looks like, why do it?**

In low-income countries, where you live your whole life without meeting a doctor, A.I. will overwhelmingly be a good thing. A woman in Nigeria who speaks Yoruba gets an experience 10 times worse than in English; we're working so she can describe her bleeding and get advice in the middle of the night. Middle-income countries will see the jobs effect after rich ones, and eventually low-income countries will too.

An economist asked me how Amazon's Mechanical Turk could still exist if A.I. was that good. I said it won't. A week later, Amazon announced it would shut down. The more you know, the more concerned you are. This is not like the PC. It's like evolutionary history: the aliens really are here, created in laboratories.

If we retain control, we reach superabundance, with deep questions of purpose but no deaths from malaria. Over a 20-year-plus transition, our job is to keep humanity in control and minimize the bioterror events and the lives damaged by job loss. The good stuff, if we're not careful, arrives more slowly than the bio, cyber, psychosocial and jobs risks, and the next five years are very significant. Stopping data centers won't slow this one iota.

**What would success look like politically?**

Two metrics: the safeguards that minimize cyber and bio risk, and the nature of the dialogue. If one party is for A.I. and the other against, we get the climate change scenario again.
