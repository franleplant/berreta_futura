Complex systems such as transportation, healthcare, and power generation are intrinsically and irreducibly hazardous, so they are heavily defended. The defenses work: operations are generally successful. Catastrophe requires several small, apparently innocuous failures joined together; a single point failure is not enough. These systems always carry latent flaws and run as broken systems, kept working by redundancy and by people. After an accident there is no isolated ‘root cause’, and hindsight makes practitioners’ gambles look like blunders. Safety is a property of the whole system, and people create it from moment to moment.

## Defended, and broken all the same

The high consequences of failure lead, over time, to layer upon layer of defense: backup systems, training, policies, certification, work rules. These shields normally divert operations away from accidents. Most failure trajectories are blocked by designed safety components; those that reach the operational level are mostly blocked, usually by practitioners.

Yet the complexity makes it impossible to run without multiple flaws present. Each is individually insufficient to cause failure, so each is regarded as minor. Eradicating them all is limited mainly by cost, and also because it is hard to see beforehand how they might contribute to an accident. The flaws change constantly with technology, work organization, and the very efforts to remove them. Accident reviews nearly always find a history of prior ‘proto-accidents’. Catastrophe is always just around the corner, and its potential cannot be eliminated.

## No root cause

Because overt failure requires multiple faults, there is no isolated cause. Each contributor is insufficient alone; only jointly are they sufficient. The search for a ‘root cause’ reflects not a technical understanding of failure but the social, cultural need to blame specific, localized forces or events.

Hindsight compounds the error. Knowing the outcome makes the warning signs seem more salient than they were, and practitioners appear to have “should have known”. *Hindsight bias remains the primary obstacle to accident investigation, especially when expert human performance is involved.*

All practitioner actions are gambles, acts taken in the face of uncertain outcomes. After accidents, *post hoc* analysis tends to regard these gambles as poor ones. That successful outcomes are also the result of gambles is not widely appreciated.

Remedies aimed at “human error” usually obstruct whatever activity seemed to “cause” the accident. They do little, since the chance of an identical accident is already extraordinarily low; the pattern of latent failures changes constantly. Instead, they usually increase the coupling and complexity of the system, adding latent failures and making accident trajectories harder to detect and block.

## The people at the sharp end

Practitioners play two roles at once: they produce, and they defend against failure. Outsiders emphasize production in quiet times and defense after accidents, and so misread the constant, simultaneous engagement with both.

Organizations are ambiguous, often intentionally, about how production targets, costs, and acceptable risks relate. All that ambiguity is resolved by actions at the sharp end. After an accident those actions may be called ‘errors’ or ‘violations’, judgments heavily biased by hindsight that ignore production pressure.

Practitioners adapt the system moment by moment: shielding vulnerable parts, concentrating resources where demand is expected, providing paths for retreat or recovery, and watching for early signs of changed performance. Their expertise is always mixed, since experts leave and trainees replace them.

Failure free operations are the work of people keeping the system within tolerable boundaries. This requires intimate contact with failure. Systems are likely to be more robust where operators can discern the “edge of the envelope”, where performance begins to deteriorate, becomes hard to predict, or cannot be readily recovered.

## Change and safety

A low accident rate may encourage changes, especially new technology, to reduce frequent, low consequence failures. These changes may create new, rare, high consequence ones, not uncommonly worse than those eliminated. They are hard to see in advance, because attention goes to the promised benefits, and because several changes may pass before the accident arrives.

Safety is an emergent property of systems. It does not reside in a person, device, or department, and it cannot be purchased or manufactured. Safety is not a thing a system has but something it is continuously doing.
