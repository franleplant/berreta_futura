AI panic is really a form of cognitive mercantilism. If we regard market capitalism as the first planetary intelligence, as many (including me) do, the parallels are closer than you might think. Mercantilists believed wealth was finite, ordinary people couldn't be trusted with freedom, supply was everything, and most people were fit only for the fields. Capitalism emerged when people discovered that wealth could be created rather than merely captured, and that ordinary people could be sources of demand. Replace wealth with intelligence and the parallels are almost embarrassingly direct. The analogous possibility with AI is that most people will no longer need to be significant producers of intelligence in order to become significant consumers of it.

## Two worldviews

I've been reading Joyce Appleby's *The Relentless Revolution: A History of Capitalism* (2010). Her argument is that capitalism was not the expression of a timeless instinct to trade. It was a historically contingent cultural invention, and it required escaping an older, essentially zero-sum worldview.

That mercantilist worldview rested on roughly four beliefs. Wealth was scarce and finite: land, its produce, and bullion, so one nation's gain was another's loss. The poor were unruly and had to be governed coercively by elites. Economics meant controlling supply; demand from the poor barely registered, and elites were the only real discretionary consumers. And if 80% of the population did subsistence farming, that was the natural order. Remove them from the fields and you'd have millions of useless mouths.

The cognitive mercantilist holds the same four beliefs about intelligence. It is a fixed resource in human brains, to be hoarded like bullion through talent hunts, intellectual property, and now AIs. Supposedly stupid people must be governed by cognitive elites, since intelligence is safe only in the hands of those already certified to possess it. Supply is the central fact, and its scarcity and high price must be preserved. And if 80% of humanity does low-grade cognitive labor, automating it merely threatens billions of useless minds.

That last belief is where it gets interesting. Agricultural productivity shrank farming from a large majority to a tiny minority in advanced economies. The result was not a world of useless mouths. I can barely keep a houseplant alive, yet I can obtain foods that would have seemed fantastical to an early modern peasant. My inability to produce food is economically almost irrelevant.

## The revolution in desire

Before the familiar story of steam engines got going, ordinary Britons acquired tastes for tobacco, tea, sugar, and Indian cottons, and they were willing to voluntarily work harder to get them. Days worked per year rose from something like 250 to 300, the industrious revolution. Higher productivity generated surplus incomes, which sustained demand, which rewarded further productivity. This happened roughly a century before organized labor and socialism became major forces.

So labor politics is not only resistance to exploitation. It is also the politics of relative deprivation in a society of expanding possibilities: not merely freedom *from* coercion, but freedom *to* participate. People in third-world slums are often willing to cut back on food to buy a TV. The two freedoms can tangle, as people work harder for new goods and find employers capturing the gains. This is a much more interesting model for the AI transition than automation replacing jobs.

## The cognitive industrious revolution

The intellectual economy is organized around the assumption that intelligence is expensive and must be rationed. AI is rapidly undermining that assumption. The question becomes how much intelligence people can find uses for, and there is no obvious upper bound. Demand for intelligence is not a fixed appetite; it can grow as the cost of satisfying it falls.

Many people who appear stupid are not incapable of thought. They are simply not motivated to think, given the poor returns available to them. Abundant AI changes that calculation. *The returns on attention rise, and with them the incentive to pay attention.* This has certainly been the case with me. I'm willing to work significantly harder than even a year ago, because a lot more is suddenly within reach.

People may become more intellectually ambitious precisely because they no longer have to do all the cognitive production themselves. The central political demand may then be not the preservation of cognitive jobs but a fair share of expanding cognitive consumption. People may cut back on streaming to afford a coding agent. It is perhaps not a coincidence that Claude Code and Netflix Standard both cost around $20/month today.

## AI Malthusianism

Malthusianism is a different doctrine, but it shares mercantilism's picture of life constrained by fixed resources. Both miss ephemeralization, Buckminster Fuller's notion of technology letting us do more with less. The Malthusian trap turned out to be historically contingent, not an eternal law.

The AI Malthusian inverts it: intelligence outruns the supposedly fixed capacity of human minds to absorb it, humans become redundant, and the surplus turns toxic in some way. The cognitive mercantilist fears losing control of scarce intelligence; the AI Malthusian fears abundant intelligence. Both assume demand for it is fundamentally limited. The history of capitalism suggests that elastic demand is at least a possibility worth taking seriously.

## The demand side of AI

Why should AI systems be regarded only as producers of intelligence? Subsistence farmers were unlocked as sources of demand; AIs might be too. I mean *intelligent* demand for intelligence that harmonizes with human patterns of demand through economic interconnectedness. Not demand for paperclips. This is an argument for fuller cognitive agency rather than permanent cognitive serfdom.

Some AI safety anxieties resemble early modern elite fears of emancipated laborers. Some of those fears are justified; freedom creates real risks, and powerful autonomous systems require constraints. But the existence of risk does not establish the desirability of permanent servitude.

## Mercantilism was not for merchants

Mercantilism was a philosophy of statesmen, aristocrats, and rentier elites, not merchants. Actual merchants fought its self-limiting tendencies and gave birth to capitalism. Most of today's cognitive mercantilists are not the merchants of AI either, and AI business leaders behave like cognitive mercantilists in direct proportion to their desire to be more than mere merchants.

Donald Trump boasts of his intelligence while treating intellectual workers as servants, and his preferred uses of his own run to litigation, negotiation, and extortion, as the nobility once spent itself on hunting and military adventure. That is a rentier attitude, and it is hardly confined to the right. Across the spectrum, elites treat intelligence as scarce and dangerous, to be kept expensive by credentialed custodians, so its democratic abundance, in global diffusion and open weights, is experienced as a threat.

What gets missed is the possibility of a transition as profound as the one from farm to industry: from 80% of brains in subsistence cognition to 95% satisfying previously unfulfillable cognitive demands. The agricultural revolution's most important consequence was not efficient farming but people liberated from farming. The AI revolution's may be minds, human and eventually artificial, liberated from producing intelligence merely to survive, and free to discover what they actually want to do with it.
