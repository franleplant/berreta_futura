Powerful AI may be opening a new age of institutional invention, and we are not yet doing the inventing. By institutions we mean the rules, roles, procedures, incentives, and organizations that structure how people coordinate: courts and elections, but also venture capital, standards bodies, contracts. AI changes them twice over, first by adding participants who differ from us in speed and replicability, then by changing what institutions themselves can do. Most attention still goes to the systems; the surrounding infrastructure is treated as fixed. Pax Machina is a publication for researchers to propose institutions suited to a world of humans and powerful AI, and for the critiques and counterproposals that test them. New governance structures will emerge whether we plan for them or not. We would rather they arrive by design.

## AI changes the institutional landscape

Our political stability depends on human limitations. A president cannot direct every department at once, so delegation and delay constrain executive power. Imagine one commanding thousands of perfectly loyal AI delegates, overseeing the machinery of state continuously and acting far faster than courts or legislatures could respond. The words of the Constitution wouldn't change, but some of its practical checks would stop working. Researchers have catalogued dozens of such failure modes.

The other half is what institutions could newly do. Voting reduces a citizen's worldview to a few multiple choice questions, partly because listening to millions and reconciling their competing concerns has historically been prohibitively expensive. Recent research suggests language models may make richer deliberation possible at scale: a model can speak with participants and help identify proposals that command far broader agreement than a fixed menu.

Many things will likely change: courts with AI judges, media with AI context and factuality, negotiations at AI speed. Some could be far better than the status quo, others far worse. The same ubiquitous monitoring could finally make public institutions accountable, or turn into an airtight surveillance state. The same agents could help people pursue their considered goals, or gradually displace human judgment.

## Institutional change has happened before

Every institution we rely on was devised in response to particular problems. Some under stress, like the post-Soviet market institutions that handed Russia over to oligarchs. Many through debate and accumulated experience. The American founders had a decade between the Declaration and the Convention to study the pre-federal state constitutions. Pennsylvania's, in 1776, had one legislative chamber and no governor's veto, so whichever faction won the annual election could pass whatever it liked. Massachusetts had two chambers, a governor with a veto, and independent judges. The Federal Constitution drew on the Massachusetts architecture of divided powers.

Institutions can also add veto points, cement incumbents, distort incentives, or decay while remaining difficult to abolish; during COVID, overlapping jurisdictions and reporting systems often gridlocked coordinated action. We take those risks as an argument for beginning early, while there is time to test new arrangements before they are direly needed.

## What Pax Machina is for

A forum for rigorous debate, drawing on mechanism design, political philosophy, social choice theory, law, economics, computer science, and AI governance. Responses are central: a proposal may be followed by a critique, an alternative design, or a revision by the original author, so the record shows ideas being proposed, contested, and refined rather than a collection of isolated essays.

We want a concrete institutional proposal, a sharp objection to one, or an analysis that changes how a class of institutions should be designed. The bar is specificity: what should change, how would it work, who holds the authority, and what could go wrong. The regulatory markets proposal is concrete enough to invite equally concrete objections, so we'd consider it in scope.

Our provisional map, the grid, organizes institutions by the scale at which they operate and the capacities they require of participants. A second view adds candidates for a world of humans and autonomous agents, illustrative and opinionated rather than exhaustive: agent-to-agent contracts, autonomous corp bylaws, market design for societies of agents, agents that adjudicate rights, geopolitical supernegotiation.

Three questions recur. How can institutions capture the benefits of AI without eroding people's ability to understand, contest, and steer the systems they depend on? How can they adapt at AI speed without losing due process, accountability, and meaningful human consent? And what properties should they demand of AI systems, given that alignment, cooperation, trustworthiness, and controllability suggest different relationships between agents, people, and institutions? ## Beginning again

Institutions often change in bursts. A crisis, breakthrough, or political shift can suddenly make reforms possible that seemed unthinkable a year before; political scientists call this a punctuated equilibrium. We cannot know when the next opening will come or who will hold power when it does. We can be certain there will be little time to design from scratch, and that the options on the table will be the ideas already developed and tested.

The liberal institutional order we inherited was itself the product of unusually bold design, radical forms of government and cooperation that now seem perfectly ordinary. It is deeply flawed and often sustained by violence, and it stands among humanity's great accomplishments. We have mostly adjusted it at the margins in recent decades, which is why reimagining it can feel impossible. But humanity has rebuilt its world many times before. Let's get to work.
