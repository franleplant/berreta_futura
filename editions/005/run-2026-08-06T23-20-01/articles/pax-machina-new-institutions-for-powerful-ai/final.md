Powerful AI will not merely be governed by institutions. It will enter them and remake them. Courts, elections, contracts, firms — all were built for creatures who tire, forget, and can occupy only one room at a time. Agents are none of these things. Yet nearly all our attention goes to the models and almost none to the scaffolding around them. Pax Machina is where that scaffolding gets drawn: concrete proposals, and the attacks on them.

## What breaks

The check on a president is not only parchment but flesh. One person cannot read every file, watch every official, sign in a thousand places at once. Give that person ten thousand loyal delegates who never sleep, and the words of the Constitution stay exactly as they were while their effect drains out of them. This is the pattern to watch for: not a law repealed, but a limitation quietly lifted.

## What opens

Voting flattens a life into a few boxes because listening to millions was too expensive. It is no longer. A model can speak with each participant and surface the proposals most people can live with. Expect the same doubling everywhere — AI judges, media that carries its own corrections, treaties negotiated at machine speed — and expect each to have a bright form and a dark one. Ubiquitous monitoring makes officials accountable or makes subjects of everyone. Agents help people pursue what they want, or slowly do the wanting for them.

## It has been done before

Steam produced the limited company, the factory inspector, the union, the schoolroom: inventions as heavy as the engine. The Americans had a decade between the Declaration and the Convention to watch the states experiment. Pennsylvania built one chamber and no veto, so whichever faction won in the spring ruled outright. Massachusetts built two chambers, a governor's veto, judges who could not be removed. The federal design copied Massachusetts. Institutions can also thicken into obstruction — COVID found the agencies tangled in each other's jurisdictions — but that is an argument for prototyping early, not for waiting.

## The grid

We keep a map of institutions arranged by the scale at which they act and the capacities they demand of those who take part. Like every catalogue, it is drawn smaller than its territory and is already behind. Beside it we keep a second map, of institutions that do not yet exist: agent-to-agent contracts, bylaws for autonomous companies, market design for societies of agents, agencies that anticipate their own regulators, geopolitical supernegotiation.

Three questions recur. How do people keep the power to understand, contest, and steer what they depend on? How can institutions move at machine speed without shedding due process and consent? And what should institutions demand of AI systems — alignment, cooperation, controllability are not the same demand, and may not be the right ones.

## What we want

Designs with the details filled in: what changes, how it works, who holds authority, what goes wrong. Not the claim that AI will upend our social infrastructure — the drawings. Critiques and counterproposals count as fully as proposals; the record of ideas being contested is the point. Write to editors@paxmachinamag.com.

## Why now

Institutions move in bursts. Something breaks, and for a short season the unthinkable becomes obvious. No one knows when that season comes or who will be holding power when it does. Only that there will be no time then to design anything; the options on the table will be whatever has already been written and tested. Let's get to work.
