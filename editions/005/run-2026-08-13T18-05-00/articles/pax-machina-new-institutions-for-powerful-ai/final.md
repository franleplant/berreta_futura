Institutions, meaning the rules, roles, procedures, incentives, and organizations by which people coordinate, are what powerful AI puts back on the drafting table. By powerful AI we mean anything from the diffusion of agents like today's to significant increases in autonomy; no breakthrough or timeline is required. Agents differ from us in speed and replicability, so institutions will at minimum have to account for them, and will also absorb AI into themselves, changing how they process information and enforce rules. Yet most collective attention goes to aligning, regulating, accelerating, or slowing the systems, while the institutional infrastructure around them is treated as fixed. Proposals are scarce and scattered. Pax Machina is a publication for researchers who will propose institutions for a world of humans and powerful AI, and for the critiques and counterproposals that test them. New governance structures will emerge whether we plan for them or not.

## What AI changes

Political stability depends on human limits. A president cannot direct every department, watch every official, or act everywhere at once, so delegation and delay constrain the office. Give that president thousands of perfectly loyal delegates, overseeing the machinery of state continuously and moving faster than courts or legislatures can answer, and the words of the constitution stay exactly as written while some of its practical checks stop working. A constitution is only as strong as the frictions it quietly assumes.

The gain runs the same way. Voting compresses a citizen's worldview into a few multiple-choice questions partly because listening to millions and reconciling their concerns has been prohibitively expensive. Recent research suggests language models may make richer large-scale deliberation possible: a model can speak with participants and help find proposals commanding far broader agreement than a fixed menu.

So the plausible futures include AI judges, media with AI factuality, negotiation at machine speed. Some could be far better than what we have, some far worse. Unprecedentedly responsive representation, or hyper-personalized manipulation. Monitoring that finally makes public institutions accountable, or an airtight surveillance state. Agents that help people pursue their considered goals, or that gradually displace human judgment.

## It has been done before

Every institution we rely on was devised against particular problems. Some were built under stress, like the post-Soviet market institutions that handed Russia to its oligarchs. Many of the durable ones were argued out. The American founders examined publicly how their republic might fail, and they had a decade between the Declaration and the Convention to watch the state constitutions work. Pennsylvania's, in 1776, had one chamber and no governor's veto, so whichever faction won the annual election could pass what it liked; Massachusetts took two chambers, a veto, and independent judges, forcing decisions through several checks. The federal draft borrowed the Massachusetts architecture.

The first industrial revolution was institutional too: the limited-liability corporation, the regulatory inspectorate, the labor union, mass public schooling. The Internet produced online auctions, reputation systems, prediction markets, community governance.

Institutions also add bureaucracy and veto points, cement incumbents, distort incentives, and decay while remaining hard to abolish; during COVID, overlapping jurisdictions and reporting systems often gridlocked coordinated action. We read those risks as a reason to start early, while arrangements can still be tested before they are direly needed. We are not approaching powerful AI with anything like the preparation the founders had. Not yet.

## What we are asking for

A forum for rigorous debate, drawing on mechanism design, political philosophy, social choice, law, economics, computer science, AI governance. Responses are central: a proposal, then a critique, an alternative, or a revision by its author, so the record shows ideas being contested rather than merely published.

The bar is specificity. Not that AI will upend our social infrastructure, but what should change, how it would work, who holds the authority, and what could go wrong. The regulatory markets proposal is concrete enough to invite equally concrete objections, and so is in scope. Our provisional map, the grid, sorts institutions by the scale at which they operate and the capacities they demand of participants; a second view sets out candidate institutions for a world of autonomous agents, illustrative and opinionated. Three questions recur: how to preserve people's ability to understand, contest, and steer the systems they depend on; how institutions can adapt at AI speed without surrendering due process, accountability, and consent; and what properties institutions should demand of AI systems, since alignment, cooperation, trustworthiness, and controllability are not the same demand.

## Beginning again

Institutions change in bursts. A crisis or a breakthrough makes thinkable in a month what was unthinkable in a year, and we cannot know when the next such opening comes or who will hold power then. We can be certain there will be little time to design from scratch, and that the options on the table will be the ideas already developed and tested.

The order we inherited was itself unusually bold design, radical forms of government that now look ordinary. Deeply flawed, often sustained by violence, and still among humanity's great accomplishments. It can be done again. Let's get to work.
