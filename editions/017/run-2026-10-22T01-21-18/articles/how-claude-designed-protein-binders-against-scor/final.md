We asked an internal research model to design minibinders against scorpion neurotoxins, hoping they might one day lead to an antivenom. Working mainly on its own for 64 hours from our prompt and four follow-ups, Claude produced a final list of designs; with our additions, 211 went to Adaptyv Bio and Twist Bioscience for testing. Seventeen met our criteria for a binder, each against a single toxin. None of the multitoxin designs met our criteria for more than one. We have not confirmed that any of these neutralize venom in a cellular environment, so we do not claim to have produced an antivenom. All 211 designs and their data are public.

### Why scorpions

Scorpions sting 1.2 million people or more a year, killing more than 3,250, mostly children in places with little access to care. The treatments that exist are made by milking venom, immunizing horses, and purifying their antibodies, a process of months. They work when given early, usually against only a few neurotoxins, and they're expensive. New ones are unlikely to earn back what they cost. Envenomation has been described as a neglected tropical disease.

Designed binders offer a possible way around that. A binder that covers the part of a toxin which latches onto a sodium channel could stop the toxin from reaching it, and such proteins can be optimized for stability and potency: useful qualities in the regions where stings happen.

### What came back from the labs

We aimed at five toxins across four regions: Cn2 (Mexico), Ts1 (Brazil), AaH I and AaH II (North Africa), and LqhIII (the Middle East). They share one fold, but any two are only 26% to 62% identical in sequence, which made them a hard test for binders meant to hit several at once.

Seven binders came out against Cn2, four against AaH II, four against AaH I, two against LqhIII. Among the 60 single-toxin designs, 11 were binders, an 18% rate consistent with typical campaigns, including our previous work. The multitoxin effort failed; the reasons could include our prompting, the limits of the available tools, or the biological feasibility of the thing itself.

Our criteria were strict: a design had to show binding in every replicate at both labs, with every KD at 1 µM or tighter, and no signal against an unrelated human protein. Our two strongest, Cn2-B1 and Cn2-B2, bound in the single-digit nanomolar range at both labs. Competition experiments suggest they attach on or near the same site as LR, an antibody reported to neutralize Cn2 in mice. That doesn't prove neutralization, but it suggests it.

### What is still missing

The results show binding against recombinant toxins, grown from genes rather than purified from venom. We tested nothing for neutralization in vitro or for efficacy in vivo. To advance Cn2-B1 we'd need to confirm it binds toxin from venom, then measure whether it stops the toxin acting on the channel, then see whether it protects mice. Beyond that a treatment must be safe, stable, affordable, and present where the stings are, which takes clinicians, funders, and policymakers.

Our two outside readers, Susana Vázquez Torres and Timothy P. Jenkins, called it a promising first step. Vázquez Torres notes that binding itself is no longer the main bottleneck, and that the real impact lies in the parts of the pipeline that come after it. Jenkins points to the speed: credible binders against five toxins in parallel, in days, in a field starved of innovation for want of commercial incentive.

Binding is the easy end of the problem, and we have only reached it.
