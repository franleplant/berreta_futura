We're opening a research preview of the Model Hardware Standard, a shared specification for AI agents to safely operate physical devices: microscopes, liquid handlers, robotic arms. Setting up a lab or a factory floor usually takes weeks or months, because most devices don't talk to each other and every bridge between them is built by hand. MHS cuts that work to hours or minutes. It works with any device with a programmable interface, is model-agnostic, and any agent harness can reach it through standard protocols such as MCP. It began as a collaboration between Anthropic and HHMI Janelia. Claude's spatial and physical reasoning is still limited, and still needs expert oversight.

## How it works

MHS introduces a standardized driver built on simple primitives: read ("get temperature"), write ("set temperature"). Devices become discoverable in a standard format, so agents and instruments find each other without a bespoke translator in between. Tags let a user state in natural language what code cannot show, the weight of a robot arm for instance, and the driver turns that into a reference file: what the device measures, what can be adjusted, what safety limits will be enforced. Agents reach the hardware three ways: MCP, the command line, and code files. When reasoning at every step would be too slow, the agent chains driver commands into a file and the devices carry it out themselves. We watched Claude nudge a laser, read the result off a camera, nudge again, then package what it had learned into a deterministic script that aligned the beam in one command.

## What our partners found

**Genentech** ran MHS as a proof-of-concept on the BCA protein assay across a liquid handler, an arm, and a plate reader. Claude first applied one flow rate to both aqueous and viscous liquids and made bubbles. Asked to optimize, it ran trial transfers with dyed liquid, read absorbance, scored itself against an expert's transfer, and settled on about 140 µL/s for water (0.016 RMSE) and 10 µL/s for BSA (0.181 RMSE), parameters their automation experts confirmed were reasonable. It recovered on its own from tip pickup and fluid detection errors. It did not grasp the physics of foam: its instinct was to retry in the same well, which made more bubbles, and researchers had to steer it to a clean well and gentler mixing. Those lessons were codified into reusable skills.

**University of Washington.** Zihao Song connected six instruments in under a week, drivers included. A dashboard replaced walking the lab; an agent watched qPCR amplification curves and halted the reaction on command; a LeRobot arm and a liquid handler traded plates without ever colliding, the arm moving about ten seconds after dispensing finished. These are still proofs of concept, and long agent runs carry compute costs to weigh against the researcher time saved.

**Carnegie Mellon** ran serial dilutions roughly three times faster. Three computers with fundamentally incompatible interfaces, a directory watcher, an old ActiveX/COM script, and a plate reader with no API at all, were presented to the model as one manifest of states and procedures. Drivers and orchestration took about eight hours, against the several weeks a vendor setup typically takes. Six induced faults, from a rotated plate to an active emergency stop, were blocked before any device moved. The agent rejected its own first curve (R² < 0.9, saturating at 200 µg/mL), reran at 100 µg/mL, and got R² > 0.98 with no human input.

**Janelia.** Virginie Ruetten's zebrafish rig ran on seven vendor programs launched in a fixed order, where getting the order wrong could cost the session. MHS puts the whole rig's state in one dictionary in shared memory, legible to any process that attaches to it. Adding a camera fell from a multi-day project to a few minutes; starting an experiment is one click; viewers and analysis are written once per data type instead of once per device. With an agent choosing where to image, she found an oscillatory population a fixed setting would have missed, and needs fewer repeat runs and fewer animals. She is still developing the framework and supervising the experiments. Physics still sets the tradeoffs.

**QuEra** aimed MHS at relocking a titanium-sapphire laser. A four-person team's bespoke script, months of work, succeeded about 58% of the time and took around 150 seconds. Four instances of Claude ran overnight, one proposing, one editing, one running the script against the live laser, one reading the log, and by morning recovery took about six seconds and worked 96% of the time. Tested afterwards with no agent involved, the finished script recovered the lock in 695 of 700 trials, 99.3%; the hardest disturbances took 10 to 14 seconds against 5 to 10 minutes for a human. Pointed next at lock quality, the agent tuned the servo's 12 PID parameters from the specialist's standing 15.7 mV of residual error down to 1.55 mV over 363 experiments and 16 unattended hours, capturing a trace and computing the full spectrum after every change rather than trusting the RMS heuristic. On a phase noise analyzer it matched the specialist's fresh manual tune across the band, except at a roughly 220 kHz resonance where the manual tune left about a thousand times more noise. Over 19 hours Claude's PIDs never lost the lock; the expert's unlocked about 1.6 times an hour. Claude could not troubleshoot the physical hardware, its understanding of the rig being programmatic rather than physical; it often paused overnight waiting for approval on anything it judged slightly risky, though an overly cautious agent is preferable to one that is not cautious enough; and the team had to supply a great deal of context.

**Tetsuwan** put MHS under ResearchOS to run qPCRs. A camera caught bubbles in master mix held by a robotic arm, ResearchOS scanned the network for a device that could help, and Claude proposed over Slack a brief low-speed spin in a centrifuge, then issued the commands. Protocols can name a force rather than a machine and let MHS convert. In a closed-loop compiler experiment of 9,143 dispenses across 300 transfer types and 1,508 conditions, the refined model predicted multi-dispense precision about 12% more accurately than the manufacturer's specification, beating it on 31 of 45 runs (p ≈ 0.001), and about 17% on the most-replicated data. Their preliminary data corroborated the watershed coalition's finding that humans are the primary contributor to fecal contamination in San Pedro Creek.

## What it doesn't do yet

Claude learns the physical world through text and images, so its spatial and physical reasoning has limits that still require expert oversight. Genentech's researchers had to teach it that foaming was a physical failure, not a software bug. MHS also doesn't yet work with hardware that has no programming interface, so we're working with those manufacturers on drivers; Hugging Face is adding support in LeRobot and Raspberry Pi is enabling integration across several products. AWS, Automata, Doosan, MBF Bioscience, QIAGEN, Tecan and Universal Robots are adding, testing, or planning support, and Danaher is exploring it with us. We'll use the preview to build safety evaluations with our launch partners and develop a physical safety roadmap, and we'll publish the findings as deployment guidance when we open source the standard. The waitlist is open.
