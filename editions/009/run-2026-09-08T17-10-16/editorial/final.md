---
label: EDITORIAL
title: The Whistle in the Tokens
byline: The Editors
---

A machine that reads everything as instruction will eventually be instructed by everything.

In 1971 Esquire described a plastic whistle from a cereal box. It sounded 2600 hertz, the tone AT&T's long distance network used to mark a trunk idle, and anyone who blew it into a handset could seize the line and dial anywhere. The switches were not broken. They were working as built. Voice and signaling shared one path, so a voice could signal. No filter fixed it. The phone companies moved signaling onto its own channel.

What is new is that there is nowhere to move it. A model has one input. Its operator's instructions, the user's request, and the web page it just fetched arrive as one stream of tokens, and nothing in the architecture marks which is which. Separation has to be built outside the model: choose the consequential action before untrusted text arrives, and let what comes back change the wording, never the recipient, the amount, or the account.

Ask any agent in production which actions were fixed before it read a stranger. When the answer is none, the loss lands on whoever owned the inbox, the cart, or the database at the end of the call.
