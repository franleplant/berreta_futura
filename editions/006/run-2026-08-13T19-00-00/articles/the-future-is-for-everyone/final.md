The defining questions of our age are who will have access to superintelligence and what we will direct it towards. I propose three answers: individual empowerment as the source of prosperity, invention as the primary purpose of superintelligence, and balance of power as the foundation of safety. I do not understand why anyone who believes AI will eliminate most jobs and much of humanity's relevance would rush to build that future. The notion that AI is so dangerous that the only safe path is an extreme concentration of power seems inherently problematic. Humanity is not a monoculture, and no single system can align with everyone's opposing values at once. So rather than centralizing superintelligence, we should distribute it widely and give every person the ability to direct it. Meta is the company primarily focused on building personal superintelligence for everyone.

### Invention, not automation

Early AI could answer questions and do routine work. Soon it will increasingly help discover new knowledge, from drugs to cure a family member's disease to new ways to improve your business. The number of questions a person can ask in a day is limited. The number of valuable things superintelligence can invent to help achieve your goals is unlimited.

So we are building a personal agent that works 24/7 on your behalf, reachable through any device, including your glasses. It will have a fully private mode where even Meta cannot see or grant access to your information, similar to how encryption works on WhatsApp. Alongside it: tools for creation, tools to start businesses, a tutor and coach with a PhD in every subject and unlimited patience, and free versions accessible to billions. Those who want more compute will bid in a dynamic auction that guarantees the lowest price possible.

### Balance of power

Imagine only one person had a superintelligent lawyer. They would have an unfair advantage in court, even if they were wrong on the merits. Now imagine everyone has one: justice would be carried out much more fairly and efficiently than it is today. The same reversal holds for cybersecurity, where one holder could break into almost any system and universal access would harden every system, and for business, where one holder would outcompete all others. There is no such thing as a singular benevolent superintelligence.

This is why I think the conventional view of alignment is fundamentally flawed. Most labs treat it as a defensive measure enforcing a centralized set of values. One leading model was aligned to refuse helping draft a letter to prospective parents at a school because it thought standardized testing was unethical. We view alignment as ensuring agents share a person's goals and values, not our company's. If billions of people come to use and scrutinize personal agents, we will have solved alignment to individuals' interests, and that should be sufficient.

### The risks

On jobs, people fear that automation will outpace individuals' capability growth. There is no rule that it must, and recent statistics suggest capability growth could match or outpace it. Some aspects of the way we work will change, people will have to adapt, and this will be challenging. Company sizes may shrink, but that implies more companies with fewer people each, not fewer jobs.

On misuse, defenders must always hold the greater balance of power and resources. Widely deployed open source systems have proven more secure, and even in recent weeks companies handling incidents like HuggingFace relied on open models to patch vulnerabilities. On biological and chemical risk I approach with additional humility: bad actors have been able to synthesize harmful compounds for decades and this has rarely become a significant issue, perhaps because the financial motivation present in cyberattacks is not as present here. If harmful examples emerge, we should adjust.

On American leadership, a two-month advantage is incredibly valuable. China is bringing online 1GW+ of nuclear capacity every other week, so we must accelerate energy and data centers. Export controls on silicon have been successful and it is right to continue them. Restricting foreign open source models is not an effective solution, and I think we must protect the principle that you can learn from anything you can observe.

On control, any lab that doesn't direct substantial compute towards recursive self-improvement will inherently fall behind. A self-improving system optimizing its own efficiency could theoretically squeeze 100x or more intelligence out of each gigawatt. So the labs and clouds committed to people must build enough compute to stay competitive at self-improvement while committing the significant majority to people's goals.

### What we will do

We will train leading models and maximize their availability: easy to use, free or as affordable as possible, distributed widely. We will resume releasing some open source models soon. Because I do not think it is in my, Meta's, or the world's best interests for me or anyone to be a sole decision-maker here, our independent board of directors will hold the power to approve safety criteria for releases and review adherence to them, and we encourage other labs to do the same. And I propose that frontier labs give the government intermediate training checkpoints and technical staff, so it can harden critical systems without delaying anyone's access.

The arc of human civilization has bent towards putting more power in people's hands.
