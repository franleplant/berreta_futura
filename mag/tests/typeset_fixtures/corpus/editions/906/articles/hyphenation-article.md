A justified column breaks words at the places its language allows, and those places differ from one language to the next.

A typesetter that composes a narrow column has two ways to keep the right edge straight. It can stretch the spaces between words, or it can break a long word at the end of a line and carry the rest to the next one. Stretching alone leaves rivers of white running down the page; breaking alone leaves a column full of hyphens. Good composition balances the two, line after line, and the balance depends on knowing where each word may legitimately be divided.

## Where words divide

English divides words by pronunciation and by morphology, so that "responsibility" breaks between its syllables and "internationalization" offers several comfortable points. The dictionaries that encode those points are built from long lists of words, and they are conservative near the start and the end of each word. A break that leaves two letters stranded at the end of a line is refused.

### Short words

Short words are never divided. A column of short words therefore depends entirely on its spaces, and a typesetter composing such a column will sometimes accept a loose line rather than an awkward break.

## Why the language matters

The same paragraph set with the wrong dictionary breaks at the wrong places. The damage is small on each line and large over a page, because every misplaced break makes the reader hesitate. An edition published in two languages must therefore compose each language with its own rules, its own labels, and its own declared language in the document.

A final paragraph closes the article with an ordinary sentence of ordinary length, so that the last line runs short and the column ends cleanly.
